# Setting Up OpenTelemetry for Distributed Logging with Grafana Loki

---

## The Big Picture: Why OpenTelemetry for Logs When You Already Have Promtail?

Before we touch a single configuration file, it is important that you understand why we are introducing the OpenTelemetry Collector into a system that already works. You have Promtail collecting logs from Docker containers, shipping them to Loki, and visualising everything in Grafana. That pipeline functions beautifully and you should be proud of it. However, it has a fundamental limitation that reveals itself the moment you ask a deceptively simple question: "What happened across all my services when this specific user request failed?"

The Promtail approach treats logs as isolated text streams. It reads the stdout of your containers, attaches structural labels like `service` and `env`, and pushes them to Loki. What it cannot do is correlate a log line from your `employee-svc` with a log line from your `leave-svc` that were both part of the same HTTP request chain. It has no concept of traces, spans, or the distributed context that ties microservices together. This is where OpenTelemetry transforms your logging from a collection of individual diaries into a unified narrative.

When you integrate the OpenTelemetry Collector into your logging pipeline, you gain three transformative capabilities. First, every log line that leaves your Go service carries a **Trace ID** and a **Span ID** — unique identifiers that connect that log line to the exact distributed trace it belongs to. Second, the OpenTelemetry Collector acts as a powerful intermediary that can receive logs, traces, and metrics from all your services through a single protocol (OTLP), process them (filter, enrich, batch, transform), and then route them to the appropriate backends — Loki for logs, Tempo or Jaeger for traces, Prometheus for metrics. Third, because your services speak OTLP (a vendor-neutral protocol), you can change your backend infrastructure — swap Loki for Elasticsearch, add Datadog alongside Grafana — without changing a single line of application code.

Think of it this way: Promtail is a postman who collects letters from mailboxes and delivers them to the post office. The OpenTelemetry Collector is a logistics hub that receives parcels from every warehouse, scans them for tracking information, sorts them by destination, and dispatches them through the optimal delivery channel. Both move packages, but the logistics hub gives you tracking, routing, and intelligence that the postman simply cannot provide.

## Chapter 1: The Mental Model — Understanding Every Piece Before You Build

Before we write any configuration, let us build a crystal-clear mental model of every component in this pipeline and the exact role it plays. If you understand the roles, every line of configuration will feel intuitive rather than magical.

**Your Go Service with Zap Logger** is the origin of all log data. You have already configured Zap to write structured JSON to stdout, and that is excellent. However, in the OpenTelemetry world, we go one step further. Instead of simply writing to stdout and hoping that some external agent (Promtail) picks up the output, your service will actively push its logs to the OpenTelemetry Collector through the OTLP protocol. This is a fundamental shift in philosophy — from passive log emission to active telemetry export. Your Zap logger will be wrapped with an OpenTelemetry "bridge" that captures every log line, attaches the current Trace ID and Span ID from the active trace context, and hands it to the OpenTelemetry SDK's log pipeline. The SDK then batches these enriched log records and exports them over gRPC to the Collector.

**The OpenTelemetry Collector** is the central nervous system of your observability infrastructure. It is a standalone binary (running as a Docker container) that receives telemetry data from your services, processes it according to your rules, and exports it to one or more backends. The Collector has three fundamental building blocks arranged in a pipeline: **Receivers** accept incoming data (in our case, OTLP data from your Go services), **Processors** transform or filter that data (batching for efficiency, adding resource attributes, dropping health-check noise), and **Exporters** send the processed data to backends (Loki for logs, Tempo for traces, Prometheus for metrics). You can think of the Collector as a programmable router for telemetry data — data flows in through receivers, passes through a chain of processors, and exits through exporters.

**Grafana Loki** remains your log storage engine. It receives logs from the Collector (instead of from Promtail) and stores them with its characteristic label-based indexing. The key difference is that logs arriving through the Collector carry richer metadata — Trace IDs, Span IDs, resource attributes — that Loki indexes as labels or structured metadata, enabling far more powerful queries.

**Grafana** remains your query and visualisation interface, but now it gains the ability to correlate logs with traces. When you view a log line in Grafana that contains a Trace ID, you can click on it and jump directly to the corresponding distributed trace in Tempo, seeing the full journey of that request across all services. This log-to-trace correlation is the holy grail of distributed debugging.

## Chapter 2: Installing the OpenTelemetry Go Log SDK Dependencies

Now that you have the mental model firmly in place, let us begin the practical work. Your Go services already have the OpenTelemetry trace SDK installed (as covered in Document 02), and Zap is already configured as your logger. What we need to add is the **log bridge** — the component that connects Zap to OpenTelemetry's log pipeline so that every log line your service writes is automatically enriched with trace context and exported to the Collector.

The OpenTelemetry Go ecosystem provides a package called `otelzap` that acts as this bridge. It wraps your existing Zap logger and creates an additional "core" that, in addition to writing logs to stdout (which you want to keep for local debugging and for Promtail backward compatibility), also sends each log record through the OpenTelemetry SDK. The SDK then attaches the Trace ID and Span ID from the current `context.Context`, batches the records, and exports them via OTLP.

Run the following commands in every Go microservice that you want to instrument. These commands add the necessary OpenTelemetry log SDK packages to your Go module:

```bash
# Core OpenTelemetry Log SDK
go get go.opentelemetry.io/otel/sdk/log

# OTLP Log Exporter (sends logs to the Collector via gRPC)
go get go.opentelemetry.io/otel/exporters/otlp/otlplog/otlploggrpc

# The Zap Bridge — connects Zap to OpenTelemetry
go get go.opentelemetry.io/contrib/bridges/otelzap

# Log API (provides the global LoggerProvider interface)
go get go.opentelemetry.io/otel/log

# You should already have these from Document 02, but verify:
go get go.opentelemetry.io/otel
go get go.opentelemetry.io/otel/sdk
go get go.opentelemetry.io/otel/exporters/otlp/otlptrace/otlptracegrpc
```

After running these commands, your `go.mod` file will contain all the dependencies needed for the complete telemetry pipeline — traces, metrics, and now logs. It is worth pausing here to appreciate the elegance of this approach. You are not installing a new logging library. You are not replacing Zap. You are adding a bridge that lets Zap continue to function exactly as it always has, while simultaneously channeling a copy of every log record through the OpenTelemetry pipeline. Your services lose nothing and gain everything.

## Chapter 3: Configuring the OpenTelemetry Log Provider in Your Go Service

In Document 02, you created a `telemetry.go` file that initialises the TracerProvider. Now we need to extend that initialisation to also create a **LoggerProvider** — the SDK component responsible for managing log record processing and export. The LoggerProvider follows the exact same pattern as the TracerProvider: you create an exporter (OTLP gRPC), wrap it in a processor (batch), attach a resource (service identity), and register it globally.

Here is the updated telemetry initialisation file. Notice how the structure mirrors the TracerProvider setup — this consistency is one of OpenTelemetry's greatest design strengths:

```go
// File: pkg/telemetry/telemetry.go
package telemetry

import (
    "context"
    "fmt"
    "time"

    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/attribute"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/exporters/otlp/otlplog/otlploggrpc"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/exporters/otlp/otlptrace/otlptracegrpc"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/log/global"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/propagation"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/sdk/resource"
    sdklog "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/sdk/log"
    sdktrace "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/sdk/trace"
    semconv "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/semconv/v1.24.0"
)

// Config holds all the configuration needed to initialise OpenTelemetry.
// In a production application, these values should come from environment
// variables or a configuration file — never hardcoded.
type Config struct {
    ServiceName    string
    ServiceVersion string
    Environment    string
    OTLPEndpoint   string // e.g. "otel-collector:4317"
}

// ShutdownFunc is a function that gracefully shuts down a provider.
type ShutdownFunc func(context.Context) error

// InitTelemetry initialises both the TracerProvider and the LoggerProvider.
// It returns a slice of shutdown functions that must be called during
// graceful shutdown to flush all pending telemetry data.
func InitTelemetry(cfg Config) ([]ShutdownFunc, error) {
    ctx := context.Background()

    // ─── Step 1: Create the Resource ───────────────────────────────────
    // A Resource describes the entity producing telemetry. Every log line,
    // every trace span, and every metric data point produced by this service
    // will carry these attributes. This is how Loki knows which service
    // a log came from, and how Grafana can filter by environment.
    res, err := resource.New(ctx,
        resource.WithAttributes(
            semconv.ServiceName(cfg.ServiceName),
            semconv.ServiceVersion(cfg.ServiceVersion),
            semconv.DeploymentEnvironment(cfg.Environment),
            attribute.String("service.team", "backend-engineering"),
        ),
        resource.WithFromEnv(),
        resource.WithProcess(),
        resource.WithOS(),
        resource.WithHost(),
    )
    if err != nil {
        return nil, fmt.Errorf("failed to create OTel resource: %w", err)
    }

    var shutdowns []ShutdownFunc

    // ─── Step 2: Initialise the TracerProvider ─────────────────────────
    // (You already have this from Document 02. Including it here for
    //  completeness so this file is self-contained.)
    traceExporter, err := otlptracegrpc.New(ctx,
        otlptracegrpc.WithEndpoint(cfg.OTLPEndpoint),
        otlptracegrpc.WithInsecure(), // Use TLS in production!
    )
    if err != nil {
        return nil, fmt.Errorf("failed to create trace exporter: %w", err)
    }

    tracerProvider := sdktrace.NewTracerProvider(
        sdktrace.WithResource(res),
        sdktrace.WithBatcher(traceExporter,
            sdktrace.WithBatchTimeout(5*time.Second),
            sdktrace.WithMaxExportBatchSize(512),
        ),
        sdktrace.WithSampler(
            sdktrace.ParentBased(sdktrace.TraceIDRatioBased(0.1)),
        ),
    )
    otel.SetTracerProvider(tracerProvider)
    shutdowns = append(shutdowns, tracerProvider.Shutdown)

    // ─── Step 3: Initialise the LoggerProvider ─────────────────────────
    // This is the NEW piece. The LoggerProvider manages the lifecycle of
    // log records — batching them in memory and exporting them to the
    // Collector via OTLP gRPC, just like the TracerProvider does for spans.
    logExporter, err := otlploggrpc.New(ctx,
        otlploggrpc.WithEndpoint(cfg.OTLPEndpoint),
        otlploggrpc.WithInsecure(), // Use TLS in production!
    )
    if err != nil {
        return nil, fmt.Errorf("failed to create log exporter: %w", err)
    }

    loggerProvider := sdklog.NewLoggerProvider(
        sdklog.WithResource(res),
        sdklog.WithProcessor(
            sdklog.NewBatchProcessor(logExporter,
                // The batch processor accumulates log records in memory
                // and flushes them to the exporter every 5 seconds, or
                // when the batch reaches 512 records — whichever comes
                // first. This dramatically reduces the number of gRPC
                // calls compared to sending each log individually.
                sdklog.WithExportTimeout(30*time.Second),
            ),
        ),
    )

    // Register the LoggerProvider globally so that the otelzap bridge
    // (which we configure next) can find it.
    global.SetLoggerProvider(loggerProvider)
    shutdowns = append(shutdowns, loggerProvider.Shutdown)

    // ─── Step 4: Configure Context Propagation ─────────────────────────
    // This tells OpenTelemetry how to encode trace context in HTTP headers.
    // W3C Trace Context is the standard format understood by all OTel SDKs.
    otel.SetTextMapPropagator(
        propagation.NewCompositeTextMapPropagator(
            propagation.TraceContext{},
            propagation.Baggage{},
        ),
    )

    return shutdowns, nil
}
```

The most important thing to notice in this code is the symmetry between the TracerProvider and the LoggerProvider. Both follow the same pattern: create an exporter, wrap it in a processor, attach a resource, and register globally. This consistency is deliberate — once you understand how to set up one signal (traces), you already understand how to set up the others (logs, metrics). The OpenTelemetry specification was designed with this uniformity in mind, and it is one of the reasons the project has achieved such widespread adoption.

## Chapter 4: Bridging Zap with OpenTelemetry Using otelzap

Now comes the most elegant part of this entire setup. You do not need to rip out your Zap logger and replace it with a new logging library. The `otelzap` bridge creates a Zap Core that, when attached to your logger, intercepts every log record and feeds a copy of it into the OpenTelemetry log pipeline. Your existing Zap configuration — the JSON encoder, the stdout output, the structured fields — continues to work exactly as before. The bridge simply adds a parallel path for the same data.

Here is how you modify your logger initialisation to include the OpenTelemetry bridge:

```go
// File: pkg/logger/logger.go
package logger

import (
    "os"

    "go.opentelemetry.io/contrib/bridges/otelzap"
    "go.uber.org/zap"
    "go.uber.org/zap/zapcore"
)

// NewProductionLogger creates a Zap logger that writes to both stdout (for
// Docker/Promtail collection) and the OpenTelemetry pipeline (for Collector
// export). This dual-write approach gives you backward compatibility with
// your existing Promtail setup while simultaneously enabling trace-correlated
// logs through OpenTelemetry.
func NewProductionLogger(serviceName string) *zap.Logger {

    // ─── Core 1: The stdout core (your existing setup) ─────────────────
    // This core writes JSON logs to stdout, exactly as before. Docker
    // captures this output, and if Promtail is still running, it will
    // continue to collect these logs. This provides a safety net during
    // the migration period — if the Collector goes down, you still have
    // logs flowing through Promtail.
    encoderCfg := zap.NewProductionEncoderConfig()
    encoderCfg.TimeKey = "timestamp"
    encoderCfg.EncodeTime = zapcore.ISO8601TimeEncoder
    encoderCfg.MessageKey = "message"
    encoderCfg.LevelKey = "level"
    encoderCfg.CallerKey = "caller"

    stdoutCore := zapcore.NewCore(
        zapcore.NewJSONEncoder(encoderCfg),
        zapcore.AddSync(os.Stdout),
        zap.InfoLevel,
    )

    // ─── Core 2: The OpenTelemetry bridge core ─────────────────────────
    // This core captures log records and sends them through the OTel SDK.
    // It does NOT write to stdout — that is Core 1's job. Instead, it
    // serialises the log record as an OTLP LogRecord and hands it to the
    // BatchLogRecordProcessor, which eventually exports it to the Collector.
    //
    // The bridge automatically reads the trace_id and span_id from the
    // context.Context that you pass to your logger. This is the magic
    // that connects your logs to your distributed traces.
    otelCore := otelzap.NewCore(serviceName)

    // ─── Combine both cores with zap.NewTee ────────────────────────────
    // zap.NewTee creates a core that writes every log record to ALL the
    // cores you give it. This means every logger.Info() call in your
    // application produces two outputs: one JSON line to stdout, and one
    // OTLP log record to the Collector. Neither knows about the other.
    combinedCore := zapcore.NewTee(stdoutCore, otelCore)

    logger := zap.New(combinedCore, zap.AddCaller()).With(
        zap.String("service", serviceName),
        zap.String("env", os.Getenv("APP_ENV")),
    )

    return logger
}
```

There is a critically important detail in this code that deserves special attention. The `otelzap` bridge core reads the Trace ID and Span ID from the `context.Context`. This means that for your logs to be properly correlated with traces, you must use the **context-aware** Zap logging methods. In vanilla Zap, you call `logger.Info("message")` without a context. But for OpenTelemetry correlation, you should use the sugared logger with context, or (more idiomatically in Go) create a logger from context in your HTTP handlers.

Here is how you use the context-aware logger in your request handlers:

```go
// File: internal/handler/employee_handler.go
package handler

import (
    "net/http"

    "go.uber.org/zap"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel"
    "go.opentelemetry.io/otel/trace"
)

type EmployeeHandler struct {
    logger *zap.Logger
}

func (h *EmployeeHandler) CreateEmployee(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
    // The request context carries the trace information injected by
    // the OTel HTTP middleware (otelhttp). When you extract the span
    // from this context, you get the Trace ID and Span ID.
    ctx := r.Context()
    span := trace.SpanFromContext(ctx)

    // Create a request-scoped logger that includes the trace identifiers.
    // Every log line written with this logger will carry these fields,
    // making it trivially easy to find all logs for a specific request.
    requestLogger := h.logger.With(
        zap.String("trace_id", span.SpanContext().TraceID().String()),
        zap.String("span_id", span.SpanContext().SpanID().String()),
    )

    requestLogger.Info("Creating new employee",
        zap.String("endpoint", "/employees"),
        zap.String("method", "POST"),
    )

    // ... your business logic here ...

    // If you create child spans for sub-operations, the otelzap bridge
    // will automatically pick up the new span_id from the context.
    tracer := otel.Tracer("employee-service")
    ctx, childSpan := tracer.Start(ctx, "validate-employee-data")
    defer childSpan.End()

    // This log line will carry the child span's span_id
    requestLogger.Info("Validating employee data",
        zap.String("span_id", childSpan.SpanContext().SpanID().String()),
    )

    // ... validation logic ...
}
```

The log output from this handler will look like this in stdout (captured by Docker, readable by Promtail), and simultaneously an identical record flows through the OTel pipeline to the Collector:

```json

    "timestamp": "2026-08-18T10:30:45.123Z",
    "level": "info",
    "caller": "handler/employee_handler.go:32",
    "message": "Creating new employee",
    "service": "employee-svc",
    "env": "production",
    "trace_id": "4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736",
    "span_id": "00f067aa0ba902b7",
    "endpoint": "/employees",
    "method": "POST"
}
```

## Chapter 5: Updating Your main.go to Wire Everything Together

Your `main.go` file needs to initialise both the telemetry providers (traces and logs) before creating the Zap logger, because the otelzap bridge requires the LoggerProvider to be registered globally before the logger is created. The ordering is important: first telemetry, then logger, then everything else.

```go
// File: cmd/employee-svc/main.go
package main

import (
    "context"
    "log"
    "net/http"
    "os"
    "os/signal"
    "syscall"
    "time"

    "your-module/pkg/telemetry"
    "your-module/pkg/logger"
    "your-module/internal/handler"

    "go.opentelemetry.io/contrib/instrumentation/net/http/otelhttp"
)

func main() {
    // ─── Step 1: Initialise OpenTelemetry ──────────────────────────────
    // This MUST happen before creating the logger, because the otelzap
    // bridge reads from the global LoggerProvider.
    shutdowns, err := telemetry.InitTelemetry(telemetry.Config{
        ServiceName:    "employee-svc",
        ServiceVersion: "1.0.0",
        Environment:    getEnv("APP_ENV", "development"),
        OTLPEndpoint:   getEnv("OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT", "otel-collector:4317"),
    })
    if err != nil {
        log.Fatalf("Failed to initialise telemetry: %v", err)
    }

    // Register shutdown functions to flush telemetry on exit
    defer func() {
        ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(context.Background(), 10*time.Second)
        defer cancel()
        for _, shutdown := range shutdowns {
            if err := shutdown(ctx); err != nil {
                log.Printf("Error during telemetry shutdown: %v", err)
            }
        }
    }()

    // ─── Step 2: Create the logger ─────────────────────────────────────
    // Now that the LoggerProvider is registered, the otelzap bridge inside
    // NewProductionLogger can connect to it.
    zapLogger := logger.NewProductionLogger("employee-svc")
    defer zapLogger.Sync()

    zapLogger.Info("Employee service starting",
        // This log line will appear both in stdout AND in the Collector
    )

    // ─── Step 3: Create handlers and routes ────────────────────────────
    employeeHandler := &handler.EmployeeHandler{
        Logger: zapLogger,
        // ... inject other dependencies ...
    }

    mux := http.NewServeMux()
    mux.HandleFunc("/employees", employeeHandler.CreateEmployee)
    mux.HandleFunc("/health", func(w http.ResponseWriter, r *http.Request) {
        w.WriteHeader(http.StatusOK)
    })

    // Wrap the entire mux with OTel HTTP middleware.
    // This creates a parent span for every incoming HTTP request and
    // injects trace context into the request's context.Context.
    otelHandler := otelhttp.NewHandler(mux, "employee-svc")

    server := &http.Server{
        Addr:    ":8080",
        Handler: otelHandler,
    }

    // ─── Step 4: Graceful shutdown ─────────────────────────────────────
    go func() {
        zapLogger.Info("HTTP server listening", zap.String("addr", ":8080"))
        if err := server.ListenAndServe(); err != nil && err != http.ErrServerClosed {
            zapLogger.Fatal("Server failed", zap.Error(err))
        }
    }()

    quit := make(chan os.Signal, 1)
    signal.Notify(quit, syscall.SIGINT, syscall.SIGTERM)
    <-quit

    zapLogger.Info("Shutting down server...")

    ctx, cancel := context.WithTimeout(context.Background(), 15*time.Second)
    defer cancel()

    if err := server.Shutdown(ctx); err != nil {
        zapLogger.Error("Server forced shutdown", zap.Error(err))
    }

    zapLogger.Info("Server exited gracefully")
}

func getEnv(key, fallback string) string {
    if v := os.Getenv(key); v != "" {
        return v
    }
    return fallback
}
```

The critical takeaway from this file is the ordering of initialisation. The telemetry providers must be created before the Zap logger, and the telemetry shutdown must happen after the HTTP server has stopped accepting requests. If you reverse this order, the otelzap bridge will have no LoggerProvider to write to during startup, and log records generated during shutdown may be lost because the exporter was already closed.

## Chapter 6: Configuring the OpenTelemetry Collector

The Collector is the heart of your new observability pipeline, and its configuration file is where the real engineering happens. The Collector configuration file is written in YAML and consists of five sections: **receivers** (how data gets in), **processors** (how data is transformed), **exporters** (where data goes out), **extensions** (health checks, authentication), and **service** (which wires receivers, processors, and exporters into named pipelines).

Let us build this configuration piece by piece, with an explanation of every decision:

```yaml
# monitoring/otel-collector/otel-collector-config.yaml

# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# RECEIVERS — How telemetry data enters the Collector
# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
receivers:

  otlp:
    protocols:
      grpc:
        endpoint: "0.0.0.0:4317"
        # The Collector listens on all interfaces at port 4317 for gRPC
        # connections. Your Go services export their telemetry here.
        # In Docker, "0.0.0.0" means "listen on all network interfaces
        # inside the container", which allows other containers on the
        # same Docker network to reach it.
        max_recv_msg_size_mib: 16
        # Maximum size of a single gRPC message. 16 MiB is generous enough
        # for large batches of logs from high-throughput services.

      http:
        endpoint: "0.0.0.0:4318"
        # The Collector also listens on port 4318 for HTTP/protobuf and
        # HTTP/JSON requests. This is useful for services that cannot use
        # gRPC (e.g., browser-based applications, or services behind
        # proxies that do not support HTTP/2).


# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# PROCESSORS — How telemetry data is transformed in-flight
# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
processors:

  batch:
    # The batch processor accumulates telemetry records and sends them
    # to exporters in batches rather than individually. This is the single
    # most important processor for production performance.
    send_batch_size: 1024
    # Flush a batch when it reaches 1024 records.
    send_batch_max_size: 2048
    # Hard cap: never send more than 2048 records in one batch.
    timeout: 5s
    # Flush whatever is in the buffer every 5 seconds, even if the batch
    # has not reached 1024 records. This ensures that low-traffic services
    # do not have their logs delayed indefinitely.

  resource:
    attributes:
      - key: deployment.environment
        action: upsert
        value: "production"
      # "upsert" means: if the attribute already exists, overwrite it;
      # if it does not exist, add it. This ensures every piece of telemetry
      # that flows through this Collector is tagged with the environment,
      # even if the sending service forgot to set it.

      - key: collector.version
        action: insert
        value: "1.0.0"
      # "insert" means: add this attribute only if it does not already
      # exist. This is metadata about the Collector itself, useful for
      # debugging when you have multiple Collectors in different versions.

  resource/loki:
    attributes:
      - key: loki.resource.labels
        action: insert
        value: "service.name, deployment.environment, service.version"
        # THIS IS CRITICALLY IMPORTANT FOR LOKI INTEGRATION.
        #
        # The Loki exporter reads this special attribute to decide which
        # resource attributes should become Loki stream labels. Without
        # this line, Loki would receive logs with no labels, making them
        # almost impossible to query.
        #
        # Only list attributes with LOW cardinality here. service.name
        # has perhaps 10 unique values (your 10 microservices). That is
        # low cardinality and perfect for a Loki label. Something like
        # "user.id" would be catastrophically high cardinality and would
        # destroy Loki's performance.

  filter/health:
    logs:
      exclude:
        match_type: regexp
        bodies:
          - ".*health.*check.*"
          - ".*readiness.*probe.*"
          - ".*liveness.*probe.*"
    # Health check logs are high-volume noise that inflate your storage
    # costs without providing debugging value. This filter processor
    # drops any log record whose body matches these patterns before
    # it reaches Loki. In a system with 10 services each receiving
    # health checks every 10 seconds, this filter can eliminate 86,400
    # worthless log lines per day.

  memory_limiter:
    check_interval: 5s
    limit_mib: 400
    spike_limit_mib: 100
    # The memory limiter is a safety valve that prevents the Collector
    # from consuming unbounded memory if your services suddenly produce
    # a massive spike of telemetry (e.g., during an error storm). When
    # memory usage exceeds 400 MiB, the Collector begins dropping data
    # to protect itself from an out-of-memory crash. The spike_limit
    # provides an additional 100 MiB headroom for transient spikes.


# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# EXPORTERS — Where processed telemetry data is sent
# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
exporters:

  otlphttp/loki:
    endpoint: "http://loki:3100/otlp"
    # Loki 3.x natively supports receiving logs via the OTLP HTTP
    # protocol. This is the recommended approach — it is simpler than
    # using the deprecated loki-specific exporter, and it means the
    # Collector speaks the same protocol to Loki that your services
    # speak to the Collector: OTLP.
    #
    # The /otlp path is Loki's OTLP ingestion endpoint. Loki translates
    # OTLP log records into its internal format, extracts labels from
    # the resource attributes (guided by loki.resource.labels), and
    # stores them as log streams.
    tls:
      insecure: true
      # In production behind a private network, this is acceptable.
      # If the Collector and Loki communicate over a public network,
      # configure proper TLS certificates here.

  otlp/tempo:
    endpoint: "tempo:4317"
    tls:
      insecure: true
    # If you have Grafana Tempo deployed for distributed tracing,
    # the Collector forwards trace spans here. This gives you the
    # complete log-to-trace correlation in Grafana: click a trace_id
    # in a log line, and Grafana opens the trace view in Tempo.
    #
    # If you are not using Tempo yet, comment out this exporter and
    # the traces pipeline in the service section below.

  debug:
    verbosity: basic
    # The debug exporter prints a summary of processed telemetry to
    # the Collector's own stdout. This is invaluable during initial
    # setup and debugging — you can see that logs are flowing through
    # the Collector without needing to check Loki. In production,
    # you might change verbosity to "basic" or remove this exporter
    # entirely to reduce Collector log volume.


# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# EXTENSIONS — Collector operational features
# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
extensions:

  health_check:
    endpoint: "0.0.0.0:13133"
    # Exposes a /health endpoint that Docker health checks can probe.
    # Returns HTTP 200 when the Collector is operational.

  zpages:
    endpoint: "0.0.0.0:55679"
    # zPages is a diagnostic UI built into the Collector. Navigate to
    # http://localhost:55679/debug/tracez in your browser to see live
    # trace data flowing through the Collector. Extremely useful for
    # debugging pipeline issues.


# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
# SERVICE — Wires receivers, processors, and exporters into pipelines
# ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
service:

  extensions: [health_check, zpages]

  pipelines:

    logs:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [memory_limiter, filter/health, resource, resource/loki, batch]
      exporters: [otlphttp/loki, debug]
      # This is the LOG pipeline. Data flows left to right:
      #
      # 1. otlp receiver accepts OTLP log records from your Go services
      # 2. memory_limiter prevents OOM (always first in the chain!)
      # 3. filter/health drops health-check noise
      # 4. resource adds/ensures deployment.environment attribute
      # 5. resource/loki sets loki.resource.labels for label extraction
      # 6. batch accumulates records for efficient export
      # 7. otlphttp/loki sends batched logs to Loki
      # 8. debug prints a summary to Collector stdout (remove in prod)

    traces:
      receivers: [otlp]
      processors: [memory_limiter, resource, batch]
      exporters: [otlp/tempo, debug]
      # This is the TRACE pipeline. Trace spans from your services
      # are forwarded to Tempo for storage and querying. If you are
      # not using Tempo, comment out this entire pipeline.

  telemetry:
    logs:
      level: info
      # The Collector's own internal logging level. Set to "debug"
      # when troubleshooting pipeline issues, then back to "info"
      # for production.
    metrics:
      address: "0.0.0.0:8888"
      # The Collector exposes Prometheus metrics about itself at this
      # address. You can scrape these with Prometheus to monitor the
      # Collector's health — how many records it processed, how many
      # it dropped, export latency, etc.
```

The pipeline architecture is the most important concept in the Collector configuration. Each pipeline is a named sequence of receivers → processors → exporters, and each pipeline handles exactly one signal type (logs, traces, or metrics). You can have multiple pipelines for the same signal type — for example, one logs pipeline that sends to Loki and another that sends to Elasticsearch — and the Collector will fan out the data accordingly. The processors are executed in the order they are listed, and this order matters. The `memory_limiter` must always be first in the chain because it needs to be able to drop data before any other processor has a chance to accumulate it in memory.

```
Collector Internal Pipeline Architecture:

                          ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
                          │          OpenTelemetry Collector              │
                          │                                              │
  OTLP Logs               │  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐ │
  from Go services ───────┤  │             LOGS Pipeline                │ │
                          │  │                                         │ │
                          │  │  otlp ─► memory_limiter ─► filter ─►   │ │
                          │  │  resource ─► resource/loki ─► batch ─► │ │
                          │  │  otlphttp/loki + debug                 │ │
                          │  │                                         │ │
                          │  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘ │
                          │                                              │
  OTLP Traces             │  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐ │
  from Go services ───────┤  │            TRACES Pipeline               │ │
                          │  │                                         │ │
                          │  │  otlp ─► memory_limiter ─► resource    │ │
                          │  │  ─► batch ─► otlp/tempo + debug        │ │
                          │  │                                         │ │
                          │  └─────────────────────────────────────────┘ │
                          │                                              │
                          │  Extensions: health_check, zpages            │
                          └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                        │                 │
                                        ▼                 ▼
                                   ┌─────────┐      ┌─────────┐
                                   │  Loki   │      │  Tempo  │
                                   │  :3100  │      │  :4317  │
                                   └─────────┘      └─────────┘
```

## Chapter 7: Updating Loki to Accept OTLP Logs

Your existing Loki configuration from Document 01 is almost production-ready. However, to accept logs from the OpenTelemetry Collector via the OTLP protocol, you need to explicitly enable the OTLP endpoint in Loki's configuration. This is a small but essential change. Without it, Loki will reject OTLP requests and the Collector's exporter will log errors.

Add or update the following section in your `loki-config.yml`:

```yaml
# monitoring/loki/loki-config.yml
# ADD these lines to your existing configuration

# ─── Enable OTLP Ingestion ─────────────────────────────────────────────
# Loki 3.x supports receiving logs via the OTLP HTTP protocol natively.
# This allows the OpenTelemetry Collector to push logs to Loki without
# needing the deprecated Loki-specific exporter.

limits_config:
  # ... keep your existing limits_config entries ...
  allow_structured_metadata: true
  # This setting is ESSENTIAL for OpenTelemetry integration. When enabled,
  # Loki stores the rich metadata from OTLP log records (trace_id, span_id,
  # severity, log attributes) as structured metadata alongside each log line.
  # Without this, Loki would discard the OTLP metadata and you would lose
  # trace correlation.

  otlp_config:
    resource_attributes:
      attributes_config:
        - action: index_label
          attributes:
            - service.name
            - deployment.environment
          # These resource attributes are promoted to Loki index labels.
          # They MUST match the attributes you listed in the Collector's
          # resource/loki processor under loki.resource.labels.
        - action: structured_metadata
          attributes:
            - service.version
            - host.name
          # These resource attributes are stored as structured metadata.
          # They are searchable but do not create new index entries,
          # keeping cardinality low.
```

The distinction between `index_label` and `structured_metadata` is the Loki equivalent of the label cardinality discussion from Document 01. Attributes promoted to `index_label` create fast-lookup index entries but must have low cardinality (service.name, environment). Attributes stored as `structured_metadata` are searchable with LogQL's metadata filters but do not inflate the index — perfect for higher-cardinality fields like host.name or service.version.

## Chapter 9: Updating Grafana Datasource for Log-to-Trace Correlation

The most powerful feature you gain from this entire setup is the ability to click on a Trace ID in a log line and jump directly to the corresponding distributed trace. For this to work, Grafana needs to know two things: where your logs are (Loki) and where your traces are (Tempo). The derived fields configuration in the Loki datasource tells Grafana how to extract Trace IDs from log lines and create clickable links to Tempo.

Update your Grafana datasource provisioning file:

```yaml
# monitoring/grafana/provisioning/datasources/datasources.yml

apiVersion: 1

datasources:

  # ─── Loki (Logs) ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  - name: Loki
    type: loki
    access: proxy
    url: http://loki:3100
    isDefault: true
    version: 1
    editable: false
    jsonData:
      maxLines: 1000
      derivedFields:
        - name: TraceID
          matcherRegex: '"trace_id":"(\w+)"'
          url: "$${__value.raw}"
          datasourceUid: tempo
          matcherType: label
          # This configuration tells Grafana: when you see a field called
          # "trace_id" in a log line, extract its value and create a
          # clickable link that opens the corresponding trace in Tempo.
          # The datasourceUid "tempo" refers to the Tempo datasource
          # defined below.

  # ─── Tempo (Traces) ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
  - name: Tempo
    type: tempo
    access: proxy
    uid: tempo
    url: http://tempo:3200
    version: 1
    editable: false
    jsonData:
      tracesToLogs:
        datasourceUid: loki
        filterByTraceID: true
        filterBySpanID: true
        # This is the reverse correlation: when viewing a trace in Tempo,
        # Grafana will show related log lines from Loki filtered by the
        # trace ID. This bidirectional linking between logs and traces is
        # what makes distributed debugging effortless.
      serviceMap:
        datasourceUid: prometheus
        # If you have Prometheus collecting metrics, Tempo can display
        # a service map showing how your services communicate. Remove
        # this section if you are not using Prometheus.
```

The `derivedFields` configuration is what creates the clickable Trace ID links in Grafana's log view. When you query logs in Grafana's Explore panel and see a log line containing `"trace_id":"4bf92f3577b34da6..."`, Grafana will automatically render the trace_id as a clickable link. Clicking it opens the Tempo trace view showing the complete distributed trace — every span, every service hop, every timing measurement — for that specific request. This is the moment where the value of the entire OpenTelemetry setup becomes viscerally clear.

## Chapter 10: Querying Trace-Correlated Logs in Grafana

With logs flowing through the OpenTelemetry Collector into Loki, your LogQL queries gain new superpowers. You can now filter and correlate logs using the trace context that OpenTelemetry automatically attaches to every log record.

Here are the most powerful queries you can now run:

```logql
# ─── Find all logs for a specific distributed trace ───────────────────
# When a user reports an issue and you have their trace_id:
{service_name="employee-svc"} | json | trace_id = "4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736"

# ─── Find all logs across ALL services for a single trace ─────────────
# This is the killer feature. One trace_id shows you the complete story
# across employee-svc, leave-svc, payroll-svc, api-gateway — everything:
{} | json | trace_id = "4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736"

# ─── Find error logs that have trace correlation ──────────────────────
# Every error log now carries a trace_id, so you can jump from the error
# to the full trace to understand the sequence of events that led to it:
{service_name="payroll-svc"} | json | level = "error" | trace_id != ""

# ─── Error rate per service (same as before, but now you can drill down):
sum by (service_name) (
  rate({deployment_environment="production"} | json | level = "error" [5m])
)

# ─── Using structured metadata for advanced filtering ─────────────────
# If you stored service.version as structured metadata in Loki:
{service_name="employee-svc"} | service_version = "2.1.0" | json | level = "error"
# This finds errors specifically in version 2.1.0 of the employee service,
# which is invaluable during canary deployments.
```

## Chapter 12: The Migration Strategy — Promtail to OpenTelemetry

Migrating from Promtail to OpenTelemetry for log collection is not something you should do all at once. A premature cutover is how production incidents are born. Instead, follow a phased approach that gives you confidence at each stage before proceeding to the next.

**Phase 1: Dual-Write (Week 1-2).** Deploy the OpenTelemetry Collector alongside Promtail. Update one service (perhaps the least critical one, like a notification service or an internal tool) to export logs via OTLP in addition to writing to stdout. During this phase, the same logs arrive in Loki through two paths — Promtail reading stdout, and the Collector receiving OTLP. Verify in Grafana that both paths produce the same log lines, and that the OTLP path includes trace_id and span_id. This is your validation period.

**Phase 2: Service Rollout (Week 2-4).** Once you trust the OTel pipeline for one service, update the remaining services one by one. After each service is updated, verify in Grafana that its logs appear through the Collector. During this phase, keep Promtail running as a safety net. If the Collector experiences an issue, Promtail continues to deliver logs.

**Phase 3: Promtail Retirement (Week 4+).** Once all services are exporting logs through the Collector and you have at least two weeks of stable operation, you can safely remove Promtail from your Docker Compose. Before doing so, ensure that your alerting rules still work, that your dashboards still populate, and that log retention is behaving correctly. After removing Promtail, your services' stdout logs are still captured by Docker's log driver (useful for `docker logs` commands during local debugging), but they are no longer shipped to Loki through that path.

```

Phase 1: Dual-Write                Phase 2: Service Rollout        Phase 3: Retirement
  (Week 1-2)                         (Week 2-4)                      (Week 4+)

  ┌─────────┐                        ┌─────────┐                     ┌─────────┐
  │ svc-1   │──► stdout ──► Promtail │ svc-1   │──► OTLP ──► OTel   │ svc-1   │──► OTLP
```

## Chapter 13: Production Hardening — What Separates a Demo from a Real System

A demo pipeline ships logs from point A to point B. A production pipeline does the same thing while surviving every failure mode that the real world throws at it — network partitions, out-of-memory crashes, disk full conditions, error storms, misconfigurations, and rolling deployments. This chapter covers the hardening measures that elevate your pipeline from a demo to a production-grade system.

**Collector Resilience.** The Collector is a single point of telemetry routing. If it goes down, your services' OTLP exporters will buffer log records in memory and retry with exponential backoff. However, if the outage is prolonged, those buffers will fill up and records will be dropped. For true production resilience, run two Collector instances behind a load balancer, or use the OpenTelemetry Collector's built-in `file_storage` extension to persist the sending queue to disk, surviving even Collector restarts:

```yaml
# Add to otel-collector-config.yaml for persistent queue

extensions:
  health_check:
    endpoint: "0.0.0.0:13133"
  file_storage/queue:
    directory: /var/lib/otelcol/queue
    # This directory must be on a persistent Docker volume. The Collector
    # will write unsent telemetry records here when exporters are
    # temporarily unavailable, and replay them when the exporter recovers.

exporters:
  otlphttp/loki:
    endpoint: "http://loki:3100/otlp"
    tls:
      insecure: true
    sending_queue:
      enabled: true
      num_consumers: 10
      queue_size: 10000
      storage: file_storage/queue
      # With persistent queue storage, even if the Collector crashes and
      # restarts, unsent logs are not lost — they are replayed from disk.
    retry_on_failure:
      enabled: true
      initial_interval: 5s
      max_interval: 30s
      max_elapsed_time: 300s
      # If Loki is temporarily unavailable, the Collector retries with
      # exponential backoff: 5s, 10s, 20s, 30s, 30s, ... up to 5 minutes.
```

**Loki Hardening.** For a production Loki deployment that handles logs from eight or more microservices, pay attention to the following settings in your `loki-config.yml`:

```yaml
# Additional production settings for loki-config.yml

limits_config:
  ingestion_rate_mb: 32
  ingestion_burst_size_mb: 64
  # Double the defaults to handle error storms where all services
  # suddenly produce high-volume error logs simultaneously.
  per_stream_rate_limit: 5MB
  per_stream_rate_limit_burst: 15MB
  # Per-stream limits prevent a single misbehaving service from
  # consuming all of Loki's ingestion capacity.
  max_query_parallelism: 16
  # Allow up to 16 parallel sub-queries when running complex LogQL
  # queries. This speeds up queries that span many streams.
  split_queries_by_interval: 1h
  # Long-range queries are split into 1-hour chunks and executed
  # in parallel. A 24-hour query becomes 24 parallel sub-queries.

storage_config:
  tsdb_shipper:
    active_index_directory: /loki/data/index
    cache_location: /loki/data/index_cache
    index_gateway_client:
      server_address: ""
  filesystem:
    directory: /loki/data/chunks

chunk_store_config:
  chunk_cache_config:
    embedded_cache:
      enabled: true
      max_size_mb: 256
    # Cache frequently accessed chunks in memory to speed up repeated
    # queries. 256 MiB is a reasonable starting point.
```

**Grafana Alerting.** Configure Grafana alerts that monitor your observability pipeline itself. If the pipeline breaks, you need to know about it before your developers notice missing logs:

```yaml
# monitoring/loki/rules/pipeline-health-alerts.yml

groups:
  - name: observability_pipeline_health
    interval: 1m
    rules:

      - alert: CollectorExportFailures
        expr: |
          sum(rate({service_name="otel-collector"} |= "export failure" [5m])) > 0
        for: 3m
        labels:
          severity: critical
        annotations:
          summary: "OTel Collector is failing to export telemetry"
          description: |
            The OpenTelemetry Collector has been logging export failures
            for 3 minutes. Loki may not be receiving logs. Check Collector
            logs and Loki health immediately.

      - alert: ServiceLogsAbsent
        expr: |
          count by (service_name) (
            sum_over_time({deployment_environment="production"} [10m])
          ) == 0
        for: 10m
        labels:
          severity: warning
        annotations:
          summary: "{{ $labels.service_name }} stopped producing logs"
          description: |
            No logs received from {{ $labels.service_name }} for 10 minutes.
            The service may have crashed or its OTel exporter may have
            lost connection to the Collector.
```

## Closing Thoughts

The pipeline you have built in this guide is not a toy demo — it is the same architectural pattern used by mature engineering teams at companies processing millions of log lines per minute. The key insight is that OpenTelemetry does not replace your existing tools; it unifies them. Your Zap logger still works. Your Loki still stores logs. Your Grafana still queries them. What OpenTelemetry adds is the connective tissue — the Trace IDs, the Span IDs, the standardised protocol, the central Collector — that transforms isolated log streams into a coherent narrative of your distributed system's behaviour. When the next production incident happens, you will not be grepping through ten separate log files hoping to find a timestamp that matches. You will paste a single Trace ID into Grafana and see the complete story, from the API gateway to the deepest database call, in one unified view.
