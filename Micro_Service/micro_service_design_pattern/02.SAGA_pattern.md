# The SAGA Pattern in Microservices

### Everything You Need to Know — Explained Like You Just Joined the Team

---

## The Problem That Made SAGA Necessary

Before we talk about what SAGA is, we need to talk about the problem it was invented to solve. Because understanding the problem deeply is what makes the solution feel obvious rather than magical.

In a monolithic application with a single database, you have access to something called a database transaction. A transaction is a guarantee from the database engine that a group of operations either all succeed together, or all fail together — with no in-between state. You have probably seen this in Go with GORM:

```go
tx := db.Begin()
tx.Create(&order)
tx.Update(&inventory)
tx.Create(&payment)
tx.Commit() // Either all three happen, or none of them do
```

If the payment creation fails halfway through, the database automatically rolls back the inventory update and the order creation. The world is left in a clean state. Nobody's inventory was decremented for an order that never got paid. This property — where a group of operations either all succeed or all fail — is called atomicity, and it is the "A" in ACID.

Now here is the painful truth about microservices: you cannot have distributed ACID transactions across multiple services. Each service has its own database. There is no single database engine sitting across all three of them that can coordinate a rollback. When your Leave Service, Employee Service, and Approval Service all need to update their respective databases as part of one logical operation, there is no built-in mechanism to guarantee that if one fails, the others undo their work.

```
THE PROBLEM: A Business Transaction Spanning 3 Services
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Step 1: Leave Service           ──► leave_applications DB  ✓  (created)
Step 2: Notification Service    ──► notifications DB        ✓  (sent)
Step 3: Approval Service        ──► approvals DB            ✗  (CRASHED!)

What happens to steps 1 and 2?
Nobody rolled them back.
The system is now in an inconsistent state.
The leave was created. The notification was sent.
But the approval record was never created.
```

This inconsistent half-finished state is one of the most dangerous things that can happen in a distributed system. Users see data that makes no sense. Billing gets confused. Support tickets flood in. On-call engineers get paged at 3am. The SAGA pattern exists specifically to prevent this.

---

## What SAGA Actually Is

SAGA is a pattern for managing long-running business transactions across multiple services, where each step in the transaction is handled by a different service with its own database. The core idea is deceptively simple: instead of one giant atomic transaction, you break the business operation into a sequence of smaller local transactions — one per service. Each local transaction updates that service's own database and then publishes an event or sends a command to trigger the next step.

The "saga" in the name is literary — it comes from Norse sagas, which were long stories made up of many episodes. Each episode in a business saga is one local transaction. The whole story must either reach a successful conclusion, or — if something goes wrong partway through — the story must be "untold" by executing a series of compensating transactions in reverse order.

```
A SAGA is a Sequence of Local Transactions
───────────────────────────────────────────

Business Goal: "Employee submits a leave application"

Local Tx 1: Leave Service creates leave record
         │
         ▼
Local Tx 2: Balance Service deducts leave balance
         │
         ▼
Local Tx 3: Notification Service sends email to manager
         │
         ▼
Local Tx 4: Approval Service creates pending approval record

Each of these is a normal, local, ACID transaction within its own DB.
Together they form one complete business-level saga.
```

The key insight that makes SAGA work is the concept of a compensating transaction. For every local transaction that can succeed, you must also define what to do if a later step fails. The compensating transaction undoes the effect of the original local transaction. It is not a database rollback — it is a new write that logically reverses the previous write.

```
COMPENSATING TRANSACTIONS
──────────────────────────

Forward Transaction                   Compensating Transaction
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Leave Service: CREATE leave record    Leave Service: CANCEL leave record
Balance Svc:   DEDUCT 3 days          Balance Svc:   RESTORE 3 days
Notification:  SEND email             Notification:  SEND cancellation email
Approval Svc:  CREATE approval        (not needed — it never succeeded)
```

The compensating transactions are executed in reverse order when a failure occurs. If Step 4 (Approval Service) fails, you run the compensating transaction for Step 3, then Step 2, then Step 1. The system ends up back in a clean state — not through a database rollback, but through actual business operations that undo the previous work.

Now there are two fundamentally different ways to coordinate these steps and compensations. These are the two types of SAGA: Choreography and Orchestration.

## SAGA Type 1 — Choreography (Event-Driven SAGA)

In Choreography, there is no central coordinator. Each service knows what it needs to do when a certain event arrives, and it knows what event to publish when its work is done. The services "dance" together according to a shared understanding of the event flow, without anyone telling them what to do.

Think of a flash mob. Nobody is standing in front giving instructions. Each participant knows the choreography in advance, watches for a cue, performs their part, and gives the next cue. The coordination emerges from the combination of individual behaviors.

```
CHOREOGRAPHY SAGA — Leave Application Flow
────────────────────────────────────────────

  Client
    │
    │  POST /leave-applications
    ▼
Leave Service
  ├── Creates leave record (local tx)
  └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.submitted"
                                   │
                         ┌─────────┘
                         ▼
              Balance Service (subscribes to "leave.submitted")
                ├── Deducts leave balance (local tx)
                └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.balance.deducted"
                                                 │
                                       ┌─────────┘
                                       ▼
                          Notification Service (subscribes to "leave.balance.deducted")
                            ├── Sends email to manager (local tx)
                            └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.notification.sent"
                                                               │
                                                     ┌─────────┘
                                                     ▼
                                        Approval Service (subscribes to "leave.notification.sent")
                                          ├── Creates approval record (local tx)
                                          └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.approval.created"
                                                                          │
                                                               ┌──────────┘
                                                               ▼
                                                        Leave Service (subscribes to "leave.approval.created")
                                                          └── Marks leave as PENDING
```

And here is what the failure path looks like in choreography. Suppose the Approval Service fails to create its record:

```
CHOREOGRAPHY SAGA — Failure and Compensation Flow
───────────────────────────────────────────────────

Approval Service (subscribes to "leave.notification.sent")
  ├── FAILS to create approval record
  └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.approval.failed"
                                    │
                          ┌─────────┘
                          ▼
             Notification Service (subscribes to "leave.approval.failed")
               ├── Sends cancellation email (compensating tx)
               └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.notification.compensated"
                                                 │
                                       ┌─────────┘
                                       ▼
                          Balance Service (subscribes to "leave.notification.compensated")
                            ├── Restores leave balance (compensating tx)
                            └── Publishes ──► Kafka: "leave.balance.compensated"
                                                             │
                                                   ┌─────────┘
                                                   ▼
                                      Leave Service (subscribes to "leave.balance.compensated")
                                        └── Marks leave as CANCELLED (compensating tx)
```

Here is the Go code for how the Balance Service would look in a choreography setup using our Kafka stack:

```go
// In Balance Service — Kafka Consumer

func (c *BalanceConsumer) HandleLeaveSubmitted(msg kafka.Message) error {
    var event LeaveSubmittedEvent
    if err := json.Unmarshal(msg.Value, &event); err != nil {
        return err
    }

    // Local ACID transaction — deduct the balance
    err := c.db.Transaction(func(tx *gorm.DB) error {
        var balance EmployeeLeaveBalance
        if err := tx.Where("employee_id = ?", event.EmployeeID).
            First(&balance).Error; err != nil {
            return err
        }

        if balance.AvailableDays < event.LeaveDays {
            // Publish failure event immediately — insufficient balance
            return c.publisher.Publish("leave.balance.failed", LeaveBalanceFailedEvent{
                LeaveID: event.LeaveID,
                Reason:  "insufficient_balance",
            })
        }

        balance.AvailableDays -= event.LeaveDays
        return tx.Save(&balance).Error
    })

    if err != nil {
        return err
    }

    // Publish success event to trigger next step
    return c.publisher.Publish("leave.balance.deducted", LeaveBalanceDeductedEvent{
        LeaveID:    event.LeaveID,
        EmployeeID: event.EmployeeID,
        DaysUsed:   event.LeaveDays,
    })
}

func (c *BalanceConsumer) HandleLeaveApprovalFailed(msg kafka.Message) error {
    var event LeaveApprovalFailedEvent
    if err := json.Unmarshal(msg.Value, &event); err != nil {
        return err
    }

    // Compensating transaction — restore the balance
    err := c.db.Transaction(func(tx *gorm.DB) error {
        var balance EmployeeLeaveBalance
        if err := tx.Where("employee_id = ?", event.EmployeeID).
            First(&balance).Error; err != nil {
            return err
        }
        balance.AvailableDays += event.DaysUsed
        return tx.Save(&balance).Error
    })

    if err != nil {
        return err
    }

    // Tell the next compensation step to proceed
    return c.publisher.Publish("leave.balance.compensated", LeaveBalanceCompensatedEvent{
        LeaveID:    event.LeaveID,
        EmployeeID: event.EmployeeID,
    })
}
```

Choreography is elegant and decoupled. No service needs to know about any other service directly — they only communicate through Kafka events. This means you can add a new service to the saga (say, an Audit Service) simply by having it subscribe to the relevant events, without touching any existing service code.

But choreography has a dark side. As the number of steps grows, the flow becomes extremely hard to follow and debug. When something goes wrong, you have to trace through a series of Kafka topics to understand what happened. There is no single place where the full picture of the saga's state lives. This is sometimes called "choreography hell."

```
CHOREOGRAPHY HELL — Hard to visualize as the saga grows

leave.submitted ──► leave.balance.deducted ──► leave.notification.sent
      │                      │                          │
      │  leave.balance.failed │  leave.notif.failed     │  leave.approval.failed
      ▼                      ▼                          ▼
leave.cancelled    leave.balance.compensated  leave.notif.compensated
                                                         │
                                             leave.balance.compensated
                                                         │
                                             leave.cancelled

Now imagine 8 services with 3 failure modes each.
Nobody can draw this on a whiteboard anymore.
```

## SAGA Type 2 — Orchestration (Command-Driven SAGA)

In Orchestration, you add a central coordinator called the Saga Orchestrator. This is a dedicated component (or service) whose only job is to know the full sequence of the saga, send commands to each service in order, wait for replies, decide what to do next, and manage compensations if anything goes wrong.

Think of an orchestra conductor. The musicians (services) do not watch each other and take cues from one another. They all watch the conductor, who tells each section exactly when to play, how loud, and for how long. The conductor has the full score (the saga definition) in front of them.

```
ORCHESTRATION SAGA — Leave Application Flow
─────────────────────────────────────────────

                    ┌─────────────────────────────┐
                    │     Leave Saga Orchestrator │
                    │  State: STARTED             │
                    │  Current Step: 1            │
                    └─────────────┬───────────────┘
                                  │
                    ┌─────────────┼──────────────┐
          Command   │             │              │(waiting)
    ┌───────────────▼──┐   ┌──────▼─────┐   ┌────▼─---─────┐
    │  Leave Service   │   │ Balance Svc│   │ Approval Svc │
    └───────────────┬──┘   └─────┬──────┘   └────┬─────--──┘
                    │            │               │
         Reply ◄──-─┘            │               │
                    │            │               │
          ┌─────────▼────────────────────────────┐
          │     Leave Saga Orchestrator          │
          │                                      │
          │  State: STEP_1_COMPLETE              │
          │  Current Step: 2                     │
          │ Sends command to Balance Service────►│
          └──────────────────────────────────────┘


The orchestrator sends one command at a time, waits for reply,
then decides the next step. It holds the full saga state.
```

In our stack, the Saga Orchestrator communicates with services through gRPC (for synchronous command-reply) or through Kafka command topics (for asynchronous command-reply). Let me show you both approaches and then explain when to use which.

Here is the full Orchestrator implementation using Kafka command topics (which fits better in our event-driven stack):

```go
// Saga State — stored in the orchestrator's own database
type LeaveSagaState struct {
    gorm.Model
    SagaID     string          `gorm:"uniqueIndex"`
    LeaveID    string
    EmployeeID string
    LeaveDays  int
    Status     SagaStatus      // STARTED, STEP1_DONE, STEP2_DONE, COMPLETED, COMPENSATING, FAILED
    CurrentStep int
}

// The Orchestrator
type LeaveSagaOrchestrator struct {
    db        *gorm.DB
    publisher KafkaPublisher
}

// Step 1: Start the saga when a leave application is requested
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) StartSaga(req LeaveApplicationRequest) error {
    sagaID := uuid.NewString()

    // Persist saga state — this is critical for recovery after crashes
    saga := &LeaveSagaState{
        SagaID:      sagaID,
        LeaveID:     req.LeaveID,
        EmployeeID:  req.EmployeeID,
        LeaveDays:   req.Days,
        Status:      SagaStatusStarted,
        CurrentStep: 1,
    }
    if err := o.db.Create(saga).Error; err != nil {
        return err
    }

    // Send command to Leave Service to create the leave record
    return o.publisher.Publish("commands.leave.create", CreateLeaveCommand{
        SagaID:     sagaID,
        LeaveID:    req.LeaveID,
        EmployeeID: req.EmployeeID,
        Days:       req.Days,
        LeaveType:  req.LeaveType,
    })
}

// Step 2: Leave Service replied success — move to balance deduction
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) HandleLeaveCreated(reply LeaveCreatedReply) error {
    var saga LeaveSagaState
    if err := o.db.Where("saga_id = ?", reply.SagaID).First(&saga).Error; err != nil {
        return err
    }

    saga.Status = SagaStatusStep1Done
    saga.CurrentStep = 2
    o.db.Save(&saga)

    return o.publisher.Publish("commands.balance.deduct", DeductBalanceCommand{
        SagaID:     saga.SagaID,
        EmployeeID: saga.EmployeeID,
        Days:       saga.LeaveDays,
    })
}

// Step 3: Balance deducted — now create the approval record
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) HandleBalanceDeducted(reply BalanceDeductedReply) error {
    var saga LeaveSagaState
    if err := o.db.Where("saga_id = ?", reply.SagaID).First(&saga).Error; err != nil {
        return err
    }

    saga.Status = SagaStatusStep2Done
    saga.CurrentStep = 3
    o.db.Save(&saga)

    return o.publisher.Publish("commands.approval.create", CreateApprovalCommand{
        SagaID:  saga.SagaID,
        LeaveID: saga.LeaveID,
    })
}

// Approval created — saga is complete!
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) HandleApprovalCreated(reply ApprovalCreatedReply) error {
    var saga LeaveSagaState
    if err := o.db.Where("saga_id = ?", reply.SagaID).First(&saga).Error; err != nil {
        return err
    }

    saga.Status = SagaStatusCompleted
    saga.CurrentStep = 0
    return o.db.Save(&saga).Error
}

// FAILURE HANDLING — Balance deduction failed, start compensation
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) HandleBalanceFailed(reply BalanceFailedReply) error {
    var saga LeaveSagaState
    if err := o.db.Where("saga_id = ?", reply.SagaID).First(&saga).Error; err != nil {
        return err
    }

    saga.Status = SagaStatusCompensating
    o.db.Save(&saga)

    // Compensate step 1 — cancel the leave record
    return o.publisher.Publish("commands.leave.cancel", CancelLeaveCommand{
        SagaID:  saga.SagaID,
        LeaveID: saga.LeaveID,
        Reason:  reply.Reason,
    })
}

// Leave cancelled (compensation done) — saga failed cleanly
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) HandleLeaveCancelled(reply LeaveCancelledReply) error {
    var saga LeaveSagaState
    if err := o.db.Where("saga_id = ?", reply.SagaID).First(&saga).Error; err != nil {
        return err
    }

    saga.Status = SagaStatusFailed
    return o.db.Save(&saga).Error
}
```

Notice that the orchestrator persists its state in its own database after every step. This is not optional — it is essential. If the orchestrator crashes in the middle of a saga, when it restarts it can look at all the in-progress sagas in the database and resume from exactly where they left off. Without persisted state, a crashed orchestrator would leave sagas frozen in a half-finished state forever.

```
ORCHESTRATION — Full Happy Path and Failure Path
──────────────────────────────────────────────────

HAPPY PATH:
───────────
Orchestrator ──► [command: create leave] ──► Leave Service
                                                   │
Orchestrator ◄── [reply: leave created] ◄──────────┘
      │
      ▼
Orchestrator ──► [command: deduct balance] ──► Balance Service
                                                      │
Orchestrator ◄── [reply: balance deducted] ◄──────────┘
      │
      ▼
Orchestrator ──► [command: create approval] ──► Approval Service
                                                       │
Orchestrator ◄── [reply: approval created] ◄───────────┘
      │
      ▼
  SAGA COMPLETE ✓


FAILURE PATH (Balance Service fails):
───────────────────────────────────────
Orchestrator ──► [command: create leave] ──► Leave Service
                                                    │
Orchestrator ◄── [reply: leave created] ◄──────────┘
      │
      ▼
Orchestrator ──► [command: deduct balance] ──► Balance Service
                                                      │
Orchestrator ◄── [reply: FAILED — insufficient] ◄─────┘
      │
      ▼  (begin compensation)
Orchestrator ──► [command: cancel leave] ──► Leave Service
                                                   │
Orchestrator ◄── [reply: leave cancelled] ◄────────┘
      │
      ▼
  SAGA FAILED CLEANLY ✗
  (system is back in consistent state)
```

## Choreography vs Orchestration — When to Use Which

This is a question every new engineer on the team will eventually ask. Both approaches implement the SAGA pattern correctly. The choice between them is an engineering judgment call based on the nature of your business transaction.

```
COMPARISON
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                    CHOREOGRAPHY              ORCHESTRATION
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Coordination        Decentralized             Central Orchestrator
How services talk   Kafka events              Kafka commands + replies (or gRPC)
Who knows the flow  Every service             Only the Orchestrator
Visibility          Low — hard to trace       High — orchestrator has full state
Debugging           Painful                   Straightforward
Coupling            Low — event-based         Medium — services know reply format
Adding a new step   Add subscriber            Modify orchestrator
Best for            Simple, linear sagas      Complex sagas with branching logic
Failure handling    Chain of compensation     Orchestrator manages compensation
State storage       Distributed / implicit    Centralized in orchestrator DB
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

A good rule of thumb is this: if the saga has fewer than four steps, all steps always run in the same linear order, and failure handling is straightforward, choreography is the lighter and more elegant choice. If the saga has five or more steps, has conditional branches (different paths based on the outcome of a step), or needs complex failure handling with partial compensations, use orchestration. In a system with 12+ services like ours, most non-trivial business transactions should use orchestration because the observability and debuggability benefits are worth the extra setup cost.

## A Real-World Complex Example — The Leave Approval Saga With Branching

Let me show you an orchestration saga that has branching logic, because real business processes almost always have conditions. In our HR system, the leave approval process works differently depending on the employee's leave type and their remaining balance. An annual leave of more than five days needs a department head to approve it, not just a direct manager.

```
LEAVE APPROVAL SAGA WITH BRANCHING LOGIC
──────────────────────────────────────────

START
  │
  ▼
[Step 1] Leave Service: Create leave record
  │
  ▼
[Step 2] Balance Service: Check and deduct balance
  │
  ├── insufficient balance? ──► [Compensate Step 1] ──► FAILED
  │
  ▼
[Step 3] Policy Service: Determine approval route
  │
  ├── days > 5? ──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                                                               │
  ▼                                                               ▼
[Step 4a] Approval Service:                            [Step 4b] Approval Service:
  Create manager approval task                           Create dept-head approval task
  │                                                               │
  └───────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┘
                              │
                              ▼
                    [Step 5] Notification Service:
                      Send email to the right approver
                              │
                              ▼
                    [Step 6] Audit Service:
                      Log the saga completion
                              │
                              ▼
                           COMPLETE ✓
```

The orchestrator handles this branching logic naturally because it is just Go code:

```go
func (o *LeaveSagaOrchestrator) HandlePolicyDecided(reply PolicyDecidedReply) error {
    var saga LeaveSagaState
    o.db.Where("saga_id = ?", reply.SagaID).First(&saga)

    saga.ApprovalRoute = reply.ApprovalRoute  // "manager" or "dept_head"
    saga.CurrentStep = 4
    o.db.Save(&saga)

    // Branch based on policy decision
    if reply.ApprovalRoute == "dept_head" {
        return o.publisher.Publish("commands.approval.create_depthead", CreateDeptHeadApprovalCommand{
            SagaID:  saga.SagaID,
            LeaveID: saga.LeaveID,
        })
    }

    return o.publisher.Publish("commands.approval.create_manager", CreateManagerApprovalCommand{
        SagaID:  saga.SagaID,
        LeaveID: saga.LeaveID,
    })
}
```

Try implementing this same branching logic with choreography. Each service would need to know the routing rules. The Notification Service would need to subscribe to both `leave.manager_approval.created` and `leave.depthead_approval.created` events and handle them separately. As conditions grow, each service becomes entangled with the business logic of the entire saga. The orchestrator approach keeps all of that logic in one place.

## The Critical Problem — Dual Write and the Outbox Pattern

There is one subtle but extremely important problem in both styles of SAGA that you will encounter in production. Look at this code carefully:

```go
func (s *LeaveService) HandleCreateLeaveCommand(cmd CreateLeaveCommand) error {
    // Write to the database
    err := s.db.Create(&Leave{
        ID:         cmd.LeaveID,
        EmployeeID: cmd.EmployeeID,
        Status:     "PENDING",
    }).Error
    if err != nil {
        return err
    }

    // Publish the reply event
    // ⚠️ What if the service crashes RIGHT HERE? ⚠️
    return s.publisher.Publish("replies.leave.created", LeaveCreatedReply{
        SagaID:  cmd.SagaID,
        LeaveID: cmd.LeaveID,
    })
}
```

The database write succeeded. Then the service crashed before publishing the Kafka message. Now the orchestrator is waiting for a reply that will never come. The leave record exists in the database, but the saga is stuck. This is called the "dual write problem" — you are writing to two different systems (database and Kafka) and there is no atomic guarantee across both.

The solution to this is the Outbox Pattern, and it is non-negotiable in production SAGA implementations.

```
THE OUTBOX PATTERN
───────────────────

Instead of writing to DB and then publishing to Kafka separately,
you write BOTH the business data AND the outgoing event into the
SAME database transaction. A separate background worker then reads
from the outbox table and publishes to Kafka.

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Leave Service Database                        │
│                                                │
│  leaves table          outbox table            │
│  ┌───────────────┐    ┌──────────────────────┐ │
│  │ id: "L001"    │    │ id: 1                │ │
│  │status: PENDING│    │ topic: replies.leave │ │
│  │ employee: 42  │    │ payload: {...}       │ │
│  └───────────────┘    │ published: false     │ │
│          ▲            └──────────────────────┘ │
│          │                      ▲              │
│          └──────────────────────┘              │
│          ONE SINGLE TRANSACTION                │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                    Outbox Worker (background goroutine)
                    polls outbox table every 100ms,
                    publishes to Kafka,
                    marks published: true
                                   │
                                   ▼
                                 Kafka
```

Here is the implementation:

```go
func (s *LeaveService) HandleCreateLeaveCommand(cmd CreateLeaveCommand) error {
    return s.db.Transaction(func(tx *gorm.DB) error {
        // Write the business data
        if err := tx.Create(&Leave{
            ID:         cmd.LeaveID,
            EmployeeID: cmd.EmployeeID,
            Status:     "PENDING",
        }).Error; err != nil {
            return err
        }

        // Write the outgoing event into the same transaction
        payload, _ := json.Marshal(LeaveCreatedReply{
            SagaID:  cmd.SagaID,
            LeaveID: cmd.LeaveID,
        })

        return tx.Create(&OutboxEvent{
            Topic:     "replies.leave.created",
            Payload:   payload,
            Published: false,
        }).Error
        // If either write fails, the whole transaction rolls back.
        // The crash-safety problem is solved.
    })
}

// Background Outbox Worker — runs as a separate goroutine
func (w *OutboxWorker) Run(ctx context.Context) {
    ticker := time.NewTicker(100 * time.Millisecond)
    for {
        select {
        case <-ctx.Done():
            return
        case <-ticker.C:
            var events []OutboxEvent
            w.db.Where("published = ?", false).
                Order("created_at asc").
                Limit(100).
                Find(&events)

            for _, event := range events {
                if err := w.publisher.Publish(event.Topic, event.Payload); err != nil {
                    // Log and retry next tick
                    continue
                }
                w.db.Model(&event).Update("published", true)
            }
        }
    }
}
```

With the Outbox Pattern in place, even if the service crashes immediately after the database transaction commits, the Outbox Worker will pick up the unpublished event on its next tick and publish it to Kafka. The saga can continue. No manual intervention needed.

## Idempotency — The Other Half of Safety

The Outbox Pattern guarantees at-least-once delivery — meaning the event will eventually be published, possibly more than once if the worker publishes it and then crashes before marking it as published. This means every service that receives commands or events in a SAGA must be idempotent — processing the same message twice must produce the same result as processing it once.

```
IDEMPOTENCY PROBLEM
─────────────────────
Outbox Worker publishes "deduct balance for leave L001"
                │
                ▼
       Balance Service receives it and deducts 3 days
                │
       Outbox Worker crashes before marking event as published
                │
                ▼
       Outbox Worker restarts and publishes the same event again
                │
                ▼
       Balance Service receives it AGAIN
       Without idempotency check: deducts ANOTHER 3 days  ← BUG!
       With idempotency check: detects duplicate, does nothing ← CORRECT
```

The implementation is straightforward — store the processed message ID in your database, and check it before processing:

```go
func (s *BalanceService) HandleDeductBalanceCommand(cmd DeductBalanceCommand) error {
    return s.db.Transaction(func(tx *gorm.DB) error {
        // Idempotency check — have we processed this command before?
        var existing ProcessedCommand
        result := tx.Where("command_id = ?", cmd.CommandID).First(&existing)
        if result.Error == nil {
            // Already processed — skip silently, this is safe
            return nil
        }

        // Do the actual work
        var balance EmployeeLeaveBalance
        if err := tx.Where("employee_id = ?", cmd.EmployeeID).First(&balance).Error; err != nil {
            return err
        }
        balance.AvailableDays -= cmd.Days
        if err := tx.Save(&balance).Error; err != nil {
            return err
        }

        // Record that we processed this command
        return tx.Create(&ProcessedCommand{
            CommandID:  cmd.CommandID,
            ProcessedAt: time.Now(),
        }).Error
    })
}
```

## One Thing Most Engineers Get Wrong About SAGA

The compensating transaction does not restore the system to the exact previous state in a way that users cannot see. It is a visible business action. When a leave application is cancelled because the balance check failed, the employee gets a "Your leave application was cancelled" email. That notification is the compensation. It is a real thing that happened in the world, not a silent database rollback.

This means you need to think about compensations from a business perspective, not just a technical one. Sometimes a compensation is straightforward — restore the balance. Sometimes it is more nuanced — you cannot "unsend" an email that was already delivered. In that case, you send a follow-up email: "Please disregard our earlier notification." The SAGA pattern gives you the framework, but the actual compensation logic must be designed by understanding the business rules.

This is also why SAGA is said to provide eventual consistency rather than strong consistency. Between the moment a step commits and the moment its compensation runs, there is a window where the system is in a partially-applied state. In our leave system, there is a brief moment where the leave record exists and the balance is deducted, but the approval record has not been created yet. This is by design and is acceptable in most business systems. Strong consistency across distributed services would require distributed locks and two-phase commits, which are orders of magnitude more complex and far less resilient under failure.

SAGA trades a tiny window of transient inconsistency for dramatic improvements in availability, scalability, and resilience — and that trade-off is almost always the right one.
