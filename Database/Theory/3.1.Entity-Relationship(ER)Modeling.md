# Entity-Relationship (ER) Modeling
### A Deep, Conceptual Journey from the Ground Up

> *ER modeling is not a database skill — it is a thinking skill. It is the art of listening to the world and drawing what you hear.*

---

## Preface: Why Modeling Comes Before Coding

Every developer's instinct, when faced with a new application, is to open a code editor and start writing. Create the database tables, define the columns, start inserting data. This instinct — the bias toward doing over thinking — is the single most common source of painful database migrations, awkward data structures, and systems that collapse under the weight of requirements they were never designed for.

Entity-Relationship modeling is the discipline of **thinking before building**. It is a structured way of understanding a domain — a business, a problem space, a real-world system — and representing that understanding as a diagram. The diagram comes before the database. The diagram is not a technical artifact; it is a **communication tool**. It is a shared language between a developer, a business analyst, a product manager, and a client, one that lets everyone verify: "Is this actually how your world works?"

The ER model was introduced by Peter Chen in his landmark 1976 paper "The Entity-Relationship Model: Toward a Unified View of Data." Chen's insight was simple and profound: the world is made of **things** (entities), those things have **characteristics** (attributes), and those things are connected to each other in **ways** (relationships). If you can identify the things, their characteristics, and their connections, you have captured the essential structure of any domain — and that structure translates directly into a database schema.

This guide will take you from the raw concepts of ER modeling all the way through diagram notation and the mechanical process of converting a diagram into actual SQL tables.

## Chapter 1: Entities, Attributes, and Relationships

### Entities: The Things That Matter

An **entity** is any object, concept, or thing in the real world that is distinguishable from other things and about which the system needs to store information. The key phrase is "needs to store information" — not every concept in the world is an entity in your system. Whether something becomes an entity depends entirely on the domain you are modeling.

Think about a university. There are many things in a university: students, professors, courses, departments, buildings, classrooms, textbooks, exam scores, library books, parking permits. Which of these become entities in a university registration system? The ones the system cares about: `Student`, `Course`, `Professor`, `Department`. A `Building` might matter if we need to know which room a class is in. A `ParkingPermit` might matter if the system also handles parking. A `Textbook` probably does not matter unless the system also handles bookstore integration.

The act of identifying entities is the act of **scoping your model** — deciding what is inside the boundary of your system and what is outside it.

```
University Registration System — Identifying Entities:

THINGS IN THE DOMAIN:
  Students        → YES, entity (system tracks their enrollment)
  Professors      → YES, entity (system tracks who teaches what)
  Courses         → YES, entity (the things being offered)
  Departments     → YES, entity (courses belong to departments)
  Buildings       → MAYBE (only if we need room assignments)
  Textbooks       → PROBABLY NOT (not in scope)
  Parking Permits → PROBABLY NOT (not in scope)

ENTITY SET:
  A single entity: "Alice Johnson, Student ID 1042"
  Entity type:     Student (the category that Alice belongs to)
  Entity set:      All students in the university (the full collection)
```

In ER modeling, we distinguish between the **entity type** (the category — like "Student") and the **entity set** (the full collection of all instances of that type). When we draw an ER diagram, we are modeling the type, not any specific instance.

An entity must be **uniquely identifiable** — there must be some way to tell one instance apart from another. Alice Johnson and Bob Smith are both students; we need something that distinguishes them. This is the role of the key attribute, which we will discuss shortly.

### Attributes: The Characteristics of Entities

An **attribute** is a property or characteristic of an entity that the system wants to record. For the `Student` entity, the relevant attributes might be `student_id`, `first_name`, `last_name`, `date_of_birth`, `email`, and `enrollment_date`. Each attribute describes one facet of what it means to be a student in this system.

Attributes come in several varieties, and distinguishing them matters for how they are eventually stored:

**Simple (Atomic) Attributes** hold a single, indivisible value. `student_id`, `email`, and `date_of_birth` are simple — they cannot be meaningfully broken down further within the system. They map directly to a single column.

**Composite Attributes** are attributes that are themselves composed of smaller parts. `full_name` might be composed of `first_name` and `last_name`. A `postal_address` might be composed of `street`, `city`, `state`, `zip_code`, and `country`. When you decide to model an attribute as composite, you are saying: "my system needs to work with the parts individually." If you only ever display the full address as a single string and never query by city or zip, there is no reason to split it — keep it simple. If you need to filter students by state, the composite breakdown is essential.

```
Composite Attribute: address
    ├── street      (e.g., "123 Main St")
    ├── city        (e.g., "Boston")
    ├── state       (e.g., "MA")
    └── zip_code    (e.g., "02101")
```

**Multi-Valued Attributes** can hold more than one value for a single entity. A `Student` might have multiple `phone_numbers` (mobile, home, work). A `Course` might have multiple `prerequisite_courses`. A `Professor` might have multiple `research_interests`. Multi-valued attributes are a signal that a separate table will be needed — they cannot be stored as a single column. They become one of the first indicators of a hidden entity or a junction table.

**Derived Attributes** are values that can be computed from other stored attributes. `age` can be derived from `date_of_birth` and today's date. `gpa` can be derived from all the grade records for a student. `duration` can be derived from `start_time` and `end_time`. Derived attributes are typically not stored in the database (to avoid redundancy and the risk of stale values); instead, they are computed at query time. In a diagram, they are noted so that the system knows they exist as a concept — but they do not become columns.

**Key Attributes** are the most important category. A **key attribute** is one whose value uniquely identifies an entity within its entity set. No two students can have the same `student_id`. No two products can have the same `sku`. The key attribute is the entity's identity — its permanent, unique fingerprint.

```
Student Entity — Attribute Classification:

  student_id       → Key attribute (unique identifier)
  first_name       → Simple attribute
  last_name        → Simple attribute
  date_of_birth    → Simple attribute
  email            → Simple attribute (also a candidate key)
  phone_numbers    → Multi-valued attribute (multiple phones allowed)
  age              → Derived attribute (computed from date_of_birth)
  address          → Composite attribute
      ├── street
      ├── city
      ├── state
      └── zip_code
```

### Relationships: The Connections Between Entities

A **relationship** is a meaningful association between two or more entity types. Relationships are not just technical connections — they encode **facts about the domain**. The relationship `Student ENROLLS_IN Course` is a fact: in the real university, students enroll in courses. The relationship `Professor TEACHES Course` is a fact: professors teach courses. Every relationship you draw should correspond to a real-world truth about the domain you are modeling.

Relationships, like entities, can have **attributes**. When a student enrolls in a course, you want to record the semester they enrolled, the grade they received, and perhaps whether they are taking it for credit or audit. These are not attributes of the student, and they are not attributes of the course — they are attributes of the **enrollment relationship itself**. The grade only makes sense in the context of "this student in this course." This is a crucial insight: when a relationship has its own data, it has attributes.

```
Relationship: Student ENROLLS_IN Course

  Relationship Attributes:
  ├── enrollment_date   (when did the student enroll?)
  ├── semester          (which semester? Fall 2024?)
  ├── grade             (what grade did they earn?)
  └── is_audit          (are they auditing, not for credit?)

  These attributes belong to the enrollment, not to Student or Course alone.
```

Relationships can involve more than two entity types. A **ternary relationship** connects three entities at once. For example, a `Project` is assigned to an `Employee` by a `Manager` — three entities, one relationship. Ternary relationships are rarer and more complex to handle, and they are often better decomposed into binary relationships, but they arise naturally in certain domains.

## Chapter 2: Cardinality — One-to-One, One-to-Many, Many-to-Many

### Cardinality: The Most Important Design Decision

Cardinality describes **how many instances of one entity can be associated with how many instances of another entity** through a given relationship. It is the single most consequential decision in ER modeling — it determines the structure of your tables, the placement of foreign keys, and whether you need junction tables. Getting cardinality wrong means rebuilding your schema later.

There are three fundamental cardinality types. We will explore each deeply, because the real skill is not just knowing the names but recognizing which one applies in a given situation.

### One-to-One (1:1)

In a one-to-one relationship, each instance of entity A is associated with **at most one** instance of entity B, and vice versa. One-to-one relationships are the rarest in practice, and when you encounter one, you should always ask: "Are these actually two entities, or are they really one entity that I have unnecessarily split?"

```
One-to-One Examples:

  Person ──────── Passport
  (one person has one passport; one passport belongs to one person)

  Employee ──────── EmployeeProfile
  (one employee has one detailed profile record)

  Country ──────── Capital_City
  (one country has one capital; one city is the capital of one country)
```

The legitimate reasons to use a 1:1 relationship rather than merging the entities into one table are: **performance** (the second entity has many columns that are rarely accessed, so you split them off to keep the main table lean), **security** (the second entity contains sensitive data — like salary — that only certain roles should access), or **optional extension** (the second entity only exists for some instances of the first — not every employee has a government security clearance, but some do, and those attributes only exist for those employees).

### One-to-Many (1:N)

In a one-to-many relationship, one instance of entity A can be associated with **many** instances of entity B, but each instance of entity B is associated with **exactly one** instance of entity A. This is by far the most common relationship in relational modeling.

```
One-to-Many Examples:

  Department ──────< Employee
  (one department has many employees; each employee belongs to one department)

  Customer ──────< Order
  (one customer places many orders; each order belongs to one customer)

  Author ──────< Book
  (one author writes many books; each book has one primary author)

  Post ──────< Comment
  (one post has many comments; each comment belongs to one post)
```

The "many" side of a 1:N relationship is where the foreign key lives. The `Employee` table gets a `department_id` column that references the `Department` table. The `Order` table gets a `customer_id` column that references the `Customer` table. This is the mechanical rule, but the conceptual truth behind it is: **the entity on the "many" side needs to remember which "one" it belongs to**.

### Many-to-Many (M:N)

In a many-to-many relationship, one instance of entity A can be associated with many instances of entity B, and one instance of entity B can also be associated with many instances of entity A.

```
Many-to-Many Examples:

  Student >──────< Course
  (one student enrolls in many courses; one course has many students)

  Product >──────< Order
  (one order contains many products; one product appears in many orders)

  Actor >──────< Movie
  (one actor appears in many movies; one movie has many actors)

  Doctor >──────< Patient
  (one doctor treats many patients; one patient sees many doctors)
```

Many-to-many relationships cannot be directly represented in a relational database with just a foreign key column. If you put `course_id` in the `Student` table, a student can only be in one course. If you put `student_id` in the `Course` table, a course can only have one student. Neither works.

The solution is a **junction table** (also called an associative table, bridge table, or linking table). The junction table has two foreign keys — one pointing to each entity — and together they form a composite primary key. The junction table represents the relationship itself as a first-class database object.

```
M:N resolved with a junction table:

  Student table    Enrollment (junction)    Course table
  +-----------+    +------------+--------+  +----------+
  | student_id|    | student_id | crs_id |  | course_id|
  | name      |◄───| (FK)       | (FK)   |──►| title    |
  | email     |    | semester   |        |  | credits  |
  +-----------+    | grade      |        |  +----------+
                   +------------+--------+
                   Primary Key: (student_id, course_id, semester)
                   Relationship attributes live here
```

The junction table is also where the **relationship attributes** live. `enrollment_date`, `grade`, and `semester` are properties of the enrollment, and so they become columns in the `Enrollment` junction table.

### Participation Constraints: Total vs. Partial

Cardinality tells you the *maximum* — how many at most. **Participation constraints** tell you the *minimum* — whether an entity must participate in a relationship or whether participation is optional.

**Total participation** (also called mandatory participation) means every instance of the entity must participate in the relationship. Every `Order` must have a `Customer` — an order without a customer is meaningless and should not exist. Every `Employee` must belong to a `Department` (in most organizations). Total participation is represented by a double line in Chen notation and a mandatory marker in Crow's Foot.

**Partial participation** (optional participation) means an entity instance *may or may not* participate. A `Customer` may or may not have placed any orders — a new customer exists in the system but has not ordered yet. A `Professor` may or may not be advising any students this semester. Partial participation is represented by a single line in Chen notation and an optional marker in Crow's Foot.

```
Cardinality + Participation combined:

  "Every order belongs to exactly one customer,
   but a customer may have placed zero or many orders."

  Customer ──── (partial, 0 or more) ────< Order ──── (total, exactly 1) ──── Customer

  In shorthand: Customer 0..* ──────── 1..1 Order
  (customer side: 0 to many)           (order side: exactly 1)
```

## Chapter 3: Weak Entities

### Entities That Cannot Stand Alone

A **weak entity** is an entity type that does not have its own key attribute — it cannot be uniquely identified by its own attributes alone. It depends on another entity (called its **identifying entity** or **owner entity**) for its existence and its identity. The relationship between a weak entity and its owner is called an **identifying relationship**.

This sounds abstract, so let us reach for a concrete example. Consider `OrderItem` — an individual line item within an order (like "3 units of Product #42"). An `OrderItem` has a `line_number` attribute (line 1, line 2, line 3 in the order), a `quantity`, and a `unit_price`. But `line_number = 2` is not unique across all order items — every order has a line 2. An `OrderItem` is only uniquely identifiable when you combine its `line_number` with the `order_id` of the `Order` it belongs to. Without the `Order`, the `OrderItem` has no meaning and no identity.

```
More Weak Entity Examples:

  Dependent (of an Employee)
  ├── A dependent child's name: "Emma"
  ├── "Emma" is not unique across all employees' dependents
  ├── Identity: Emma + Employee #1042 (her parent)
  └── Owner: Employee

  OrderItem (of an Order)
  ├── Line number: 3
  ├── Line 3 exists in every order
  ├── Identity: Line 3 + Order #5521
  └── Owner: Order

  Room (of a Building)
  ├── Room number: 204
  ├── Room 204 exists in every building
  ├── Identity: Room 204 + Building "Engineering Hall"
  └── Owner: Building
```

The **partial key** (sometimes called a discriminator) is the attribute of a weak entity that, combined with the owner's key, forms a unique identity. `line_number` is the partial key of `OrderItem`. Alone it is not unique; together with `order_id`, it uniquely identifies one item in the entire system.

Weak entities have two important implications for database design. First, a weak entity's table must always include a foreign key to its owner's table, and that foreign key is part of the weak entity's primary key (it participates in the composite primary key). Second, weak entities have **total participation** in their identifying relationship — an `OrderItem` cannot exist without an `Order`. If the `Order` is deleted, the `OrderItem` must also be deleted (cascade delete).

```
Database representation of a Weak Entity:

  orders table              order_items table (weak entity)
  +----------+              +----------+-----------+----------+-------+
  | order_id |◄─────────── | order_id | line_num  | qty      | price |
  | total    |  identifying | (FK,PK)  | (PK)      |          |       |
  | date     |  relationship+----------+-----------+----------+-------+
  +----------+
                            Primary Key: (order_id, line_num)
                            order_id is BOTH a FK and part of the PK
```

## Chapter 4: ER Diagrams — Chen Notation vs. Crow's Foot

### Why Two Notations Exist

Peter Chen introduced the original ER notation in 1976, and it remains the clearest for learning because its symbols have direct visual correspondence to the concepts (rectangles look like records, diamonds look like junctions, ovals look like attributes). But Chen notation becomes visually cluttered for large real-world schemas.

Crow's Foot notation (developed by Gordon Everest in the 1970s and popularized by tools like ERwin and Lucidchart) is more compact and is the standard in industry tools today. It drops the attribute ovals entirely (attributes are listed inside the entity box), and it represents cardinality and participation with compact markers on the relationship lines. Most professional diagramming tools — draw.io, dbdiagram.io, Mermaid — use Crow's Foot.

### Chen Notation: The Original, Concept-First Approach

In Chen notation, each concept has its own distinct shape:

```
CHEN NOTATION SYMBOLS:

  ┌─────────────┐
  │   ENTITY    │   Rectangle → an entity type
  └─────────────┘

  ┌─────────────┐
  ║ WEAK ENTITY ║   Double rectangle → a weak entity type
  └─────────────┘

     ◇                Diamond → a relationship
   ◇   ◇
     ◇

     ╬                Double diamond → identifying relationship (for weak entities)

    (   )              Oval → an attribute

   ((   ))             Double oval → multi-valued attribute

   (- - -)             Dashed oval → derived attribute

   (───┬───)           Oval with internal structure → composite attribute
       ├── (part 1)
       └── (part 2)

  ─── underline ───    Underline inside attribute oval → key attribute
```

A complete Chen diagram for a simple library system looks like this:

```
CHEN NOTATION — Library System (ASCII representation):

   (member_id)  (name)  (email)
       │           │       │
       ▼           ▼       ▼
  ┌─────────────────────────┐
  │         MEMBER          │
  └─────────────┬───────────┘
                │
                │  M
                ◇
             BORROWS         ─── (borrow_date)
                ◇            ─── (due_date)
                │
                │  N
  ┌─────────────┴───────────┐
  │          BOOK           │
  └─────────────────────────┘
   (isbn)   (title)   (author)

  Cardinality labels:
  M on the MEMBER side = many members can borrow
  N on the BOOK side   = many books can be borrowed
  → This is a M:N relationship
```

For weak entities and identifying relationships:

```
CHEN NOTATION — Weak Entity (Order and OrderItem):

  ┌──────────────────┐
  │      ORDER       │
  └────────┬─────────┘
           │ 1
           ╬              ← Double diamond = identifying relationship
        CONTAINS
           ╬
           │ N
  ╔══════════════════╗
  ║    ORDER_ITEM    ║    ← Double rectangle = weak entity
  ╚══════════════════╝
  (line_num)  (qty)  (price)
  (─line_num─) ← dashed underline = partial key
```

### Crow's Foot Notation: The Industry Standard

Crow's Foot notation represents entities as rectangles with the entity name at the top and attributes listed inside. Relationships are lines connecting entities, with special symbols at each end indicating cardinality and participation. The symbols resemble animal tracks, hence the name.

```
CROW'S FOOT SYMBOLS (relationship line endings):

  CARDINALITY (maximum):
  ──|     exactly one (the vertical bar)
  ──<     many (the crow's foot — three prongs)
  ──○     zero (the circle)

  PARTICIPATION (minimum):
  ──|     mandatory (one vertical bar = at least one)
  ──○     optional (circle = zero allowed)

  COMBINED (cardinality + participation read from entity outward):
  ──|──|     exactly one (mandatory one-to-one)
  ──○──|     zero or one (optional one — "may have one")
  ──|──<     one or many (mandatory many — "at least one")
  ──○──<     zero or many (optional many — "may have many")

  How to read: standing at the entity, look OUTWARD along the line
  The symbols nearest the OTHER entity tell you how many of
  the other entity this entity relates to.
```

The same library system in Crow's Foot notation:

```
CROW'S FOOT NOTATION — Library System:

  ┌──────────────────┐                    ┌──────────────────┐
  │ MEMBER           │                    │ BOOK             │
  ├──────────────────┤                    ├──────────────────┤
  │ PK member_id     │                    │ PK isbn          │
  │    name          │                    │    title         │
  │    email         │                    │    author        │
  │    join_date     │                    │    genre         │
  └────────┬─────────┘                    └──────────┬───────┘
           │                                         │
           │ ○──<  BORROWS  >──○                     │
           └─────────────────────────────────────────┘

  Reading left side (from MEMBER outward):
    ○ = zero (a member may have zero borrows)
    < = many (a member may have many borrows)
    → A member borrows ZERO OR MANY books

  Reading right side (from BOOK outward):
    ○ = zero (a book may not be borrowed right now)
    < = many (the same book title can be borrowed by many members over time)
    → A book is borrowed by ZERO OR MANY members
```

A richer Crow's Foot diagram showing a full e-commerce domain:

```
CROW'S FOOT NOTATION — E-Commerce System:

  ┌──────────────────┐          ┌──────────────────────┐
  │ CUSTOMER         │          │ ORDER                │
  ├──────────────────┤          ├──────────────────────┤
  │ PK customer_id   │          │ PK order_id          │
  │    name          │          │ FK customer_id       │
  │    email         │          │    order_date        │
  │    phone         │          │    status            │
  └────────┬─────────┘          │    total_amount      │
           │                    └──────────┬───────────┘
           │ ──|     places    >──○        │
           └────────────────────────────── ┘
        (one customer, mandatory)  (zero or many orders, optional)


  ┌──────────────────────┐     ┌──────────────────────┐     ┌─────────────────┐
  │ ORDER                │     │ ORDER_ITEM           │     │ PRODUCT         │
  ├──────────────────────┤     ├──────────────────────┤     ├─────────────────┤
  │ PK order_id          │──|─<│ PK,FK order_id       │>─|──│ PK product_id   │
  │    order_date        │     │ PK line_number       │     │    name         │
  │    total             │     │ FK product_id        │     │    price        │
  └──────────────────────┘     │    quantity          │     │    stock_qty    │
                               │    unit_price        │     └─────────────────┘
                               └──────────────────────┘
  ORDER ──|─< ORDER_ITEM: one order has one or many items (mandatory many)
  PRODUCT ──|─< ORDER_ITEM: one product appears in one or many items (mandatory)
  ORDER_ITEM is a weak entity (PK includes FK order_id)


  ┌──────────────────┐          ┌──────────────────────┐
  │ PRODUCT          │          │ CATEGORY             │
  ├──────────────────┤          ├──────────────────────┤
  │ PK product_id    │>─○──|───│ PK category_id       │
  │    name          │          │    name              │
  │    price         │          │    description       │
  │ FK category_id   │          └──────────────────────┘
  └──────────────────┘
  Many products belong to one category (mandatory one on the category side)
  A category may have zero or many products (optional many on the product side)
```

### Mermaid ER Diagrams (Code-Based Crow's Foot)

In modern development, ER diagrams are often written as code rather than drawn by hand. **Mermaid** is a popular text-based diagramming language that renders Crow's Foot ER diagrams and integrates with GitHub, Notion, and many documentation tools.

```
erDiagram
    CUSTOMER {
        int     customer_id PK
        string  name
        string  email
        string  phone
    }
    ORDER {
        int     order_id    PK
        int     customer_id FK
        date    order_date
        string  status
        decimal total_amount
    }
    ORDER_ITEM {
        int     order_id    PK, FK
        int     line_number PK
        int     product_id  FK
        int     quantity
        decimal unit_price
    }
    PRODUCT {
        int     product_id  PK
        string  name
        decimal price
        int     stock_qty
        int     category_id FK
    }
    CATEGORY {
        int     category_id PK
        string  name
        string  description
    }

    CUSTOMER  ||--o{  ORDER       : "places"
    ORDER     ||--|{  ORDER_ITEM  : "contains"
    PRODUCT   ||--|{  ORDER_ITEM  : "included in"
    CATEGORY  ||--o{  PRODUCT     : "classifies"
```

```
Mermaid cardinality symbols:
  ||   exactly one
  |{   one or many
  o{   zero or many
  |o   zero or one

  So: ||--o{  means "exactly one to zero or many" (standard 1:N)
      ||--|{  means "exactly one to one or many" (mandatory on both sides)
```

## Chapter 5: Converting ER to Relational Schema

### The Bridge from Concept to Database

An ER diagram is a conceptual model — it exists in the world of ideas. A relational schema is a logical model — it exists in the world of tables, columns, keys, and constraints. Converting from one to the other is a mechanical process with clear rules for every case. Once you know the rules, the conversion is almost algorithmic.

There are six cases to handle. Master these six, and you can convert any ER diagram to a complete SQL schema.

### Rule 1: Regular Entities Become Tables

Every regular (non-weak) entity type becomes a table. The entity's name becomes the table name. Each simple attribute becomes a column. The key attribute becomes the primary key. Composite attributes are flattened — each component becomes its own column. Multi-valued attributes are handled separately (Rule 5). Derived attributes are not stored (they are computed at query time).

```
ER: STUDENT entity
    Attributes: student_id (key), first_name, last_name,
                date_of_birth, email,
                address (composite: street, city, state, zip)

↓ Converts to:

SQL:
  CREATE TABLE students (
      student_id    SERIAL          PRIMARY KEY,
      first_name    VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
      last_name     VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
      date_of_birth DATE            NOT NULL,
      email         VARCHAR(255)    NOT NULL UNIQUE,
      -- composite address flattened into components:
      street        VARCHAR(255),
      city          VARCHAR(100),
      state         CHAR(2),
      zip_code      VARCHAR(10)
  );
  -- age (derived) is NOT stored; computed as:
  -- EXTRACT(YEAR FROM AGE(NOW(), date_of_birth))
```

### Rule 2: Weak Entities Become Tables with Composite Primary Keys

A weak entity becomes a table, just like a regular entity. But its primary key is a **composite key** made up of:
1. The foreign key referencing the owner entity's primary key
2. The weak entity's own partial key (discriminator)

The foreign key is also given `ON DELETE CASCADE` because the weak entity cannot exist without its owner.

```
ER: ORDER_ITEM (weak entity, owner: ORDER)
    Partial key: line_number
    Attributes: quantity, unit_price

↓ Converts to:

SQL:
  CREATE TABLE order_items (
      order_id     INT              NOT NULL,
      line_number  INT              NOT NULL,
      product_id   INT              NOT NULL REFERENCES products(product_id),
      quantity     INT              NOT NULL CHECK (quantity > 0),
      unit_price   NUMERIC(10, 2)  NOT NULL,

      PRIMARY KEY (order_id, line_number),  -- composite PK
      FOREIGN KEY (order_id)
          REFERENCES orders(order_id)
          ON DELETE CASCADE                 -- weak entity vanishes with owner
  );
```

### Rule 3: One-to-Many Relationships — Foreign Key in the "Many" Side

A 1:N relationship does not need a new table. It is represented by placing a foreign key in the table on the "many" side, pointing to the primary key of the table on the "one" side. The "many" side entity "remembers" which "one" it belongs to.

Whether the foreign key column is `NOT NULL` depends on the participation constraint:
- Total participation (mandatory): `NOT NULL` — every row must reference an owner
- Partial participation (optional): nullable — the column can be `NULL`

```
ER: DEPARTMENT (1) ──────< EMPLOYEE (N)
    Every employee belongs to exactly one department.
    A department may have zero or many employees.

↓ Converts to:

  departments table:               employees table:
  ┌──────────────────┐             ┌──────────────────────────┐
  │ PK department_id │◄────────── │ FK department_id NOT NULL│
  │    name          │             │ PK employee_id           │
  │    location      │             │    name                  │
  └──────────────────┘             │    hire_date             │
                                   └──────────────────────────┘

SQL:
  CREATE TABLE departments (
      department_id  SERIAL        PRIMARY KEY,
      name           VARCHAR(100)  NOT NULL,
      location       VARCHAR(100)
  );

  CREATE TABLE employees (
      employee_id    SERIAL        PRIMARY KEY,
      department_id  INT           NOT NULL,  -- NOT NULL = total participation
      name           VARCHAR(100)  NOT NULL,
      hire_date      DATE          NOT NULL,
      FOREIGN KEY (department_id) REFERENCES departments(department_id)
  );
```

### Rule 4: Many-to-Many Relationships Become Junction Tables

An M:N relationship always requires a new table — the junction (or bridge) table. This table has two foreign keys, one for each entity, and together they form the composite primary key. Any attributes on the relationship itself become columns in the junction table.

```
ER: STUDENT (M) >──────────< COURSE (N)
    Relationship: ENROLLMENT
    Relationship attributes: semester, grade, enrollment_date, is_audit

↓ Converts to:

SQL:
  CREATE TABLE students (
      student_id   SERIAL        PRIMARY KEY,
      name         VARCHAR(100)  NOT NULL,
      email        VARCHAR(255)  NOT NULL UNIQUE
  );

  CREATE TABLE courses (
      course_id    SERIAL        PRIMARY KEY,
      title        VARCHAR(200)  NOT NULL,
      credits      SMALLINT      NOT NULL DEFAULT 3
  );

  -- Junction table: represents the ENROLLMENT relationship
  CREATE TABLE enrollments (
      student_id      INT           NOT NULL,
      course_id       INT           NOT NULL,
      semester        VARCHAR(20)   NOT NULL,  -- 'Fall2024', 'Spring2025'
      enrollment_date DATE          NOT NULL DEFAULT CURRENT_DATE,
      grade           CHAR(2),                 -- NULL until graded
      is_audit        BOOLEAN       NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE,

      PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_id, semester),
      -- semester is part of the PK: a student can retake a course in a later semester
      FOREIGN KEY (student_id) REFERENCES students(student_id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
      FOREIGN KEY (course_id)  REFERENCES courses(course_id)   ON DELETE RESTRICT
  );
```

Notice the design decision in the primary key: `(student_id, course_id, semester)` rather than just `(student_id, course_id)`. This allows a student to retake a course in a different semester. The choice of primary key encodes a **business rule** — and that rule must come from the domain, not from the database modeler's assumption.

### Rule 5: Multi-Valued Attributes Become Separate Tables

A multi-valued attribute cannot be stored in a single column. It becomes its own table with a foreign key back to the entity it belongs to. The new table's primary key is the combination of the entity's primary key and the multi-valued attribute value.

```
ER: EMPLOYEE entity
    Multi-valued attribute: phone_numbers

↓ Converts to:

SQL:
  -- Instead of a phone_numbers column in employees:
  CREATE TABLE employee_phone_numbers (
      employee_id   INT          NOT NULL,
      phone_number  VARCHAR(20)  NOT NULL,
      phone_type    VARCHAR(20)  DEFAULT 'mobile',  -- 'mobile', 'home', 'work'

      PRIMARY KEY (employee_id, phone_number),
      FOREIGN KEY (employee_id)
          REFERENCES employees(employee_id)
          ON DELETE CASCADE
  );
```

### Rule 6: One-to-One Relationships — Foreign Key with UNIQUE Constraint

A 1:1 relationship can be handled in two ways. The most common is to place the foreign key in one of the two tables (typically the "optional" or "extension" side) and add a `UNIQUE` constraint on the foreign key column. The `UNIQUE` constraint enforces the "at most one" part — if two rows in the employee_profiles table pointed to the same employee, that would violate the 1:1 constraint.

```
ER: EMPLOYEE (1) ──────── EMPLOYEE_CLEARANCE (0..1)
    (An employee may or may not have a security clearance on file)

↓ Converts to:

SQL:
  CREATE TABLE employees (
      employee_id  SERIAL        PRIMARY KEY,
      name         VARCHAR(100)  NOT NULL
  );

  CREATE TABLE employee_clearances (
      clearance_id     SERIAL        PRIMARY KEY,
      employee_id      INT           NOT NULL UNIQUE,  -- UNIQUE enforces 1:1
      clearance_level  VARCHAR(20)   NOT NULL,         -- 'SECRET', 'TOP SECRET'
      granted_date     DATE          NOT NULL,
      expires_date     DATE,

      FOREIGN KEY (employee_id) REFERENCES employees(employee_id) ON DELETE CASCADE
  );
  -- UNIQUE on employee_id: no two clearance records can point to the same employee
```

Alternatively, for 1:1 relationships where both sides always exist together, you can simply merge the two entities into one table. The decision between merging and keeping them separate depends on whether the second entity's attributes are always present (merge) or optionally present (keep separate, as above).

## Chapter 6: A Complete End-to-End Example

### From Domain Description to SQL Schema

Let us walk through the entire process — domain understanding, entity identification, ER diagram, and schema conversion — for a realistic scenario: a hospital appointment system.

**Domain Description:** A hospital has multiple departments (cardiology, orthopedics, etc.). Each department has many doctors. Each doctor belongs to exactly one department. Patients register with the hospital once and can book appointments with multiple doctors. Each appointment is with exactly one doctor and exactly one patient. An appointment has a date, a time slot, and notes written by the doctor. A doctor can have many appointments; a patient can have many appointments. Each patient has a primary contact phone number and may have additional emergency contact phone numbers.

**Step 1 — Identify Entities:**

```
Entities identified:
  DEPARTMENT  — the hospital's organizational units
  DOCTOR      — physicians who see patients
  PATIENT     — people who receive care
  APPOINTMENT — a scheduled visit (has its own attributes → entity, not just relationship)
```

**Step 2 — Identify Attributes and Keys:**

```
DEPARTMENT:  department_id (key), name, floor_number
DOCTOR:      doctor_id (key), first_name, last_name, specialty, license_number
PATIENT:     patient_id (key), first_name, last_name, date_of_birth, email,
             primary_phone (simple), emergency_phones (multi-valued)
APPOINTMENT: appointment_id (key), appointment_date, time_slot,
             status ('scheduled', 'completed', 'cancelled'),
             doctor_notes (derived? no — stored, written by doctor)
```

**Step 3 — Identify Relationships and Cardinality:**

```
DEPARTMENT ──────< DOCTOR
  One department has many doctors.
  Every doctor belongs to exactly one department.
  Cardinality: 1:N
  Participation: Doctor → total (must belong to a dept)
                 Department → partial (may have no doctors yet)

DOCTOR >──────────< PATIENT (through APPOINTMENT)
  One doctor sees many patients (over time).
  One patient sees many doctors (over time).
  But every appointment has exactly one doctor and one patient.
  → APPOINTMENT acts as a named junction entity (it has its own attributes)
  Cardinality: DOCTOR 1:N APPOINTMENT, PATIENT 1:N APPOINTMENT

PATIENT ─── emergency_phones (multi-valued attribute)
```

**Step 4 — ER Diagram (Crow's Foot, Mermaid syntax):**

```
erDiagram
    DEPARTMENT {
        int    department_id PK
        string name
        int    floor_number
    }
    DOCTOR {
        int    doctor_id      PK
        int    department_id  FK
        string first_name
        string last_name
        string specialty
        string license_number
    }
    PATIENT {
        int    patient_id    PK
        string first_name
        string last_name
        date   date_of_birth
        string email
        string primary_phone
    }
    PATIENT_EMERGENCY_PHONES {
        int    patient_id   PK, FK
        string phone_number PK
        string contact_name
    }
    APPOINTMENT {
        int    appointment_id   PK
        int    doctor_id        FK
        int    patient_id       FK
        date   appointment_date
        string time_slot
        string status
        text   doctor_notes
    }

    DEPARTMENT    ||--o{  DOCTOR                   : "employs"
    DOCTOR        ||--o{  APPOINTMENT              : "conducts"
    PATIENT       ||--o{  APPOINTMENT              : "attends"
    PATIENT       ||--|{  PATIENT_EMERGENCY_PHONES : "has"
```

**Step 5 — Full SQL Schema:**

```sql
-- Rule 1: Regular entities become tables

CREATE TABLE departments (
    department_id   SERIAL          PRIMARY KEY,
    name            VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL UNIQUE,
    floor_number    SMALLINT
);

CREATE TABLE doctors (
    doctor_id       SERIAL          PRIMARY KEY,
    department_id   INT             NOT NULL,              -- total participation
    first_name      VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
    last_name       VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
    specialty       VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
    license_number  VARCHAR(50)     NOT NULL UNIQUE,

    FOREIGN KEY (department_id)
        REFERENCES departments(department_id)
        ON DELETE RESTRICT                                 -- cannot delete dept with doctors
);

CREATE TABLE patients (
    patient_id      SERIAL          PRIMARY KEY,
    first_name      VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
    last_name       VARCHAR(100)    NOT NULL,
    date_of_birth   DATE            NOT NULL,
    email           VARCHAR(255)    NOT NULL UNIQUE,
    primary_phone   VARCHAR(20)     NOT NULL               -- simple attribute
);

-- Rule 5: Multi-valued attribute → separate table

CREATE TABLE patient_emergency_phones (
    patient_id      INT             NOT NULL,
    phone_number    VARCHAR(20)     NOT NULL,
    contact_name    VARCHAR(100),

    PRIMARY KEY (patient_id, phone_number),               -- composite PK
    FOREIGN KEY (patient_id)
        REFERENCES patients(patient_id)
        ON DELETE CASCADE                                  -- phones vanish with patient
);

-- Rule 3/4: APPOINTMENT resolves the DOCTOR–PATIENT M:N
-- (it is a named junction entity with its own attributes and its own PK)

CREATE TYPE appointment_status AS ENUM ('scheduled', 'completed', 'cancelled');

CREATE TABLE appointments (
    appointment_id      SERIAL              PRIMARY KEY,
    doctor_id           INT                 NOT NULL,
    patient_id          INT                 NOT NULL,
    appointment_date    DATE                NOT NULL,
    time_slot           VARCHAR(20)         NOT NULL,     -- e.g., '09:00–09:30'
    status              appointment_status  NOT NULL DEFAULT 'scheduled',
    doctor_notes        TEXT,                             -- NULL until appointment done

    FOREIGN KEY (doctor_id)
        REFERENCES doctors(doctor_id)
        ON DELETE RESTRICT,
    FOREIGN KEY (patient_id)
        REFERENCES patients(patient_id)
        ON DELETE RESTRICT,

    -- A doctor cannot have two appointments at the same date and time slot
    UNIQUE (doctor_id, appointment_date, time_slot)
);

-- Indexes for common query patterns
CREATE INDEX idx_appointments_doctor    ON appointments (doctor_id, appointment_date);
CREATE INDEX idx_appointments_patient   ON appointments (patient_id, appointment_date);
CREATE INDEX idx_doctors_department     ON doctors (department_id);
```

### Reading the Schema Back into English

A mark of a well-designed schema is that it reads back into the business rules that produced it:

- The `UNIQUE (doctor_id, appointment_date, time_slot)` constraint reads: "A doctor cannot be double-booked in the same time slot on the same day."
- The `ON DELETE RESTRICT` on `doctors.department_id` reads: "You cannot delete a department that still has doctors."
- The `ON DELETE CASCADE` on `patient_emergency_phones.patient_id` reads: "When a patient record is deleted, all their emergency contacts are automatically removed."
- The `NOT NULL` on `doctors.department_id` reads: "Every doctor must belong to a department."
- The nullable `doctor_notes` column reads: "Notes are optional and will be empty until after the appointment."

Every constraint is a business rule encoded directly into the database, enforced automatically, forever.

## Epilogue: The Map Is Not the Territory

An ER diagram is a map of a domain. Like all maps, it is a simplification — it captures the most important structures and omits the details that do not matter for the purpose at hand. A subway map leaves out hills and buildings; an ER diagram leaves out implementation details and edge cases.

The skill of ER modeling is not the skill of drawing shapes. It is the skill of **asking the right questions**. What are the things that matter in this domain? What do we need to know about each thing? How do the things relate to each other? Can a student be in the same course twice? (Cardinality and key design.) Can an order exist without a customer? (Participation constraint.) Is "room number" unique across all buildings, or only within a building? (Weak entity recognition.)

These questions, and their answers, are where the real work of database design happens. The diagram is just the record of the answers.

When you sit down with a stakeholder and draw an ER diagram together, you are not doing a technical task — you are doing a discovery task. The diagram forces both of you to be precise about things that were previously vague. It surfaces assumptions, reveals contradictions, and exposes ambiguity before any code is written. Every hour spent on a good ER model saves ten hours of painful schema migrations later.

Draw the diagram. Ask the questions. Get the answers right. Then let the schema follow naturally.
