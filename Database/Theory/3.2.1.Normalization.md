# Database Normalization
### A Deep, Conceptual Journey from the Ground Up

> *Normalization is not a set of rules to memorize — it is a way of listening to your data until it tells you how it wants to be organized.*

## Preface: The World Before Normalization

Imagine you have just been hired as a developer at a small company. The company has been tracking its orders in a single spreadsheet — one giant table with every piece of information crammed into it. Before you write a single line of code, your manager hands you a printout of this table:

```
ORDER_DATA (the original mess):

+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+----------+-------------------+---------+----------+
| order_id | cust_id| cust_name        | cust_email       | zip | prod_ids | prod_names        | qtys    | salesman |
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+----------+-------------------+---------+----------+
| 1001     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| P1, P2   | Widget, Gadget    | 2, 1    | Bob King |
| 1002     | C02    | Bob Smith        | bob@email.com    |90210| P1       | Widget            | 5       | Alice Ray|
| 1003     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| P3       | Doohickey         | 1       | Bob King |
| 1004     | C03    | Carol White      | carol@email.com  |10001| P1, P3   | Widget, Doohickey | 3, 2    | Bob King |
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+----------+-------------------+---------+----------+
```

Take a moment to really look at this table. Even at a glance, you can feel that something is deeply wrong. Products are crammed into a single cell as a comma-separated list. Alice Johnson's name and email appear twice. If Bob King gets married and changes his name, you have to find and update every row he ever touched. If you delete order 1002, you lose all knowledge that Bob Smith was ever a customer.

These feelings of wrongness are not aesthetic preferences. They are symptoms of a precisely defined set of problems that the theory of normalization names, categorizes, and provides a cure for. This guide is about understanding those problems so deeply that normalization feels not like a set of arbitrary rules, but like the only logical response to the chaos in that spreadsheet.

## Chapter 1: What Is Normalization and Why Do We Need It

### The Core Idea

Normalization is the process of **organizing a relational database schema to reduce data redundancy and improve data integrity**. It works by decomposing one large, problematic table into smaller, well-structured tables — and defining relationships between them — such that the same information is stored in only one place, and the structure of the tables faithfully reflects the real-world relationships in the domain.

The word "normalize" literally means "to bring into conformance with a standard." In mathematics, normalizing a vector means adjusting it to have unit length while preserving its direction. In database design, normalizing a table means adjusting its structure to eliminate problems while preserving all the information it contained. Nothing is lost; it is only reorganized.

The theory of normalization was developed by Edgar F. Codd — the same researcher who invented the relational model — in a series of papers in the early 1970s. He defined a series of **Normal Forms** (1NF, 2NF, 3NF, BCNF, 4NF, 5NF), each one building on the previous, each one eliminating a more subtle class of redundancy. In practice, achieving 3NF (and understanding why each step was necessary) is sufficient for the vast majority of real-world database design.

### The Three Anomalies: What Normalization Cures

Before we learn what normalization does, we must deeply understand what it cures. There are three types of problems that arise in poorly structured (unnormalized) tables, and they all go by the name **anomalies** — because they are situations where the data behaves in surprising, contradictory, or destructive ways when you try to modify it.

**The Update Anomaly** occurs when a single real-world fact is stored in multiple rows, so updating that fact requires changing multiple rows — and if you miss even one, your database becomes internally inconsistent. In the ORDER_DATA table above, Alice Johnson's email is stored in rows 1001 and 1003. If Alice changes her email, you must remember to update both rows. If you update only row 1001, the database now contains two different email addresses for the same customer. Both cannot be right. The database has become inconsistent with itself — and there is no way, from inside the database, to know which one is correct.

```
Update Anomaly Example:

Alice changes her email from alice@email.com to alice@newdomain.com.

Developer updates row 1001 but forgets row 1003.

Result:
  Row 1001: cust_email = alice@newdomain.com   ← updated
  Row 1003: cust_email = alice@email.com       ← stale, forgotten

  Which email is correct? The database cannot tell you.
  Both rows claim to describe Alice Johnson.
  The database has contradicted itself.
```

**The Insertion Anomaly** occurs when you cannot record a piece of information without simultaneously recording another piece of information that does not yet exist. In the ORDER_DATA table, suppose a new customer — Dave — signs up for the service but has not placed any orders yet. Can you record Dave's existence? You would need to insert a row into ORDER_DATA, but ORDER_DATA is an orders table — every row must have an `order_id`. You would be forced to either invent a fake order or leave the order fields NULL, polluting the orders table with a row that is not actually an order. The information about Dave's existence is **trapped** — it cannot be stored until it is accompanied by order information.

```
Insertion Anomaly Example:

New customer Dave registers but places no order.

To store Dave, you must insert into ORDER_DATA, which requires:
  order_id:  ??? (no order exists yet — must be NULL or fake)
  prod_ids:  ??? (no products — NULL)
  qtys:      ??? (no quantities — NULL)

The table structure makes it IMPOSSIBLE to record a customer
without simultaneously recording an order.
Dave's existence cannot be represented in this design.
```

**The Deletion Anomaly** is the mirror image of the insertion anomaly. It occurs when deleting a row causes you to inadvertently lose information about an entirely different real-world fact. In the ORDER_DATA table, Bob Smith (C02) has placed only one order — order 1002. If order 1002 is cancelled and deleted, the row disappears — and with it, all knowledge that Bob Smith ever existed as a customer. His name, email, and customer ID are gone. Deleting an order accidentally deleted a customer.

```
Deletion Anomaly Example:

Order 1002 is cancelled and its row is deleted.

Gone forever:
  cust_id:    C02
  cust_name:  Bob Smith
  cust_email: bob@email.com

Bob Smith has been silently erased from the database.
Deleting one business fact (a cancelled order) accidentally
destroyed an entirely unrelated business fact (a customer's existence).
```

These three anomalies — update, insertion, and deletion — are not bugs in your application code. They are **structural properties of the table design itself**. No amount of careful application programming fully prevents them as long as the underlying table structure is flawed. The only cure is to fix the structure, which is exactly what normalization does.

### The Decomposition Principle

Normalization cures anomalies through **decomposition** — splitting one table into multiple smaller tables, each of which represents exactly one concept, where each concept is stored in exactly one place. The key insight is that the information is not lost; it is separated into the table where it logically belongs, and the tables are linked through foreign keys.

After proper normalization, the ORDER_DATA table would become:

```
customers    → stores customer facts (name, email) — once, in one place
orders       → stores order facts (date, customer reference)
products     → stores product facts (name, price) — once, in one place
order_items  → stores the line items (which product, which order, quantity)
salespeople  → stores salesperson facts — once, in one place
```

Alice's email now lives in exactly one row in the `customers` table. Updating it requires changing exactly one row. Bob Smith can exist in `customers` without any orders. Deleting an order from `orders` leaves `customers` completely untouched.

## Chapter 2: Functional Dependencies

### The Mathematical Heart of Normalization

To understand normalization precisely — not just intuitively — you need to understand **functional dependencies**. This is the mathematical concept that underpins every normal form. Once you grasp functional dependencies, the normal forms are not arbitrary rules; they are logical consequences of what the data is actually saying.

A **functional dependency** exists between two attributes (or sets of attributes) A and B — written as **A → B** (read as "A determines B" or "B is functionally dependent on A") — when knowing the value of A is sufficient to determine exactly one value of B. In other words, if two rows have the same value for A, they must also have the same value for B. No exceptions.

### Understanding "Determines"

The word "determines" is crucial and often misunderstood. It does not mean "A causes B" or "A is related to B." It means something very specific: **given any value of A, there is at most one corresponding value of B in the database**. The constraint goes in one direction.

Let us make this concrete. In a well-designed database:

`student_id → student_name` is a functional dependency because for any given student ID, there is exactly one student name. If you know the ID is 1042, you know the name is "Alice Johnson" — there is no ambiguity, no row where ID 1042 has a different name.

`student_id → email` is a functional dependency for the same reason — each student has exactly one email address in the system.

But `student_name → student_id` is NOT a functional dependency — two different students could share the same name ("John Smith"), so knowing the name does not uniquely determine the ID.

And `zip_code → city` is a functional dependency (in the US, each ZIP code maps to exactly one city or locality), but `city → zip_code` is NOT (a city like Boston has many ZIP codes).

```
Functional Dependency Notation:

  A → B   means:  knowing A tells you exactly one B
                  (A "determines" B)

Examples:
  student_id   → student_name     ✓ (each ID → one name)
  student_id   → email            ✓ (each ID → one email)
  order_id     → order_date       ✓ (each order → one date)
  order_id     → customer_id      ✓ (each order → one customer)
  zip_code     → city             ✓ (each zip → one city/locality)
  isbn         → book_title       ✓ (each ISBN → one title)

  student_name → student_id       ✗ (two students can share a name)
  city         → zip_code         ✗ (a city has many zip codes)
  customer_id  → order_date       ✗ (a customer has many orders,
                                      each with different dates)
```

### Composite Determinants

A functional dependency can have a **composite determinant** — a set of attributes that together determine another attribute, even though neither attribute alone does.

`(order_id, product_id) → quantity` is a functional dependency because knowing both the order AND the product tells you exactly how many units of that product were ordered. But `order_id → quantity` is NOT a dependency (an order has many products, each with its own quantity), and `product_id → quantity` is NOT a dependency (a product appears in many orders, each with different quantities). Only the combination of both attributes determines quantity.

```
Composite Determinants:

  (order_id, product_id) → quantity         ✓ (both together → one qty)
  (student_id, course_id) → grade           ✓ (both together → one grade)
  (employee_id, project_id) → hours_worked  ✓ (both together → one value)

  order_id → quantity      ✗ (many products per order, each different qty)
  product_id → quantity    ✗ (same product in many orders, different qtys)
```

### The Key as a Special Functional Dependency

A **primary key** is precisely defined by functional dependencies: the primary key is the set of attributes that **functionally determines all other attributes** in the table. If K is the primary key, then K → every other column. This is the definition of a key: it uniquely identifies a row, which means knowing K tells you everything about that row.

A **candidate key** is any minimal set of attributes that could serve as the primary key — minimal meaning that if you removed any attribute from the set, it would no longer determine all others. Every table has at least one candidate key (the primary key), and may have more (like `email` alongside `student_id` — both uniquely identify a student, so both are candidate keys, and you must choose one to be the primary key).

### Trivial and Non-Trivial Dependencies

A functional dependency A → B is called **trivial** if B is a subset of A. For example, `(student_id, name) → student_id` is trivial — of course knowing both the ID and the name tells you the ID; the ID was already part of what you knew. Trivial dependencies always hold and are uninteresting for normalization purposes. We care about **non-trivial** dependencies, where B is not a subset of A.

### Transitive Dependencies

One more concept before we proceed to the normal forms: a **transitive dependency** is an indirect dependency. If A → B and B → C, then we can conclude A → C — but the dependency from A to C is indirect, passing through B. This indirectness is what creates a specific class of problems that 3NF addresses.

```
Transitive Dependency:

  student_id → zip_code        (a student has one zip code)
  zip_code   → city            (a zip code determines a city)
  ─────────────────────────────
  student_id → city            (transitive: student → city, via zip)

  The city is determined by the student ID, but only INDIRECTLY —
  through the intermediate fact of the zip code.
  This indirectness is the problem 3NF will cure.
```

## Chapter 3: First Normal Form (1NF)

### The Starting Point: What Makes a Table a Table?

First Normal Form addresses the most fundamental problems — the ones that violate the very definition of a relational table. Before we can talk about functional dependencies between columns, the columns themselves must contain individual, atomic, meaningful values. A column full of comma-separated lists is not a column in any meaningful relational sense — it is a miniature spreadsheet crammed into a cell.

### The Two Requirements of 1NF

A table is in **First Normal Form** if and only if:

**Requirement 1: All attribute values are atomic.** Every cell in the table must contain a single, indivisible value. Not a list, not a set, not an array, not a comma-separated string of multiple values. One cell, one value. This is the **atomicity** requirement — the word comes from the Greek "atomos," meaning indivisible.

**Requirement 2: There are no repeating groups.** A repeating group is a set of columns that conceptually represents the same thing, repeated multiple times across columns (like `phone1`, `phone2`, `phone3`). Repeating groups, like non-atomic values, violate the spirit of relational structure — they represent a hidden array that belongs in a separate table.

Let us return to our ORDER_DATA table and diagnose its 1NF violations:

```
ORDER_DATA — 1NF Violations:

+----------+--------+------------------+----------+-------------------+---------+
| order_id | cust_id| cust_name        | prod_ids | prod_names        | qtys    |
+----------+--------+------------------+----------+-------------------+---------+
| 1001     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | P1, P2   | Widget, Gadget    | 2, 1    |
| 1002     | C02    | Bob Smith        | P1       | Widget            | 5       |
| 1003     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | P3       | Doohickey         | 1       |
| 1004     | C03    | Carol White      | P1, P3   | Widget, Doohickey | 3, 2    |
+----------+--------+------------------+----------+-------------------+---------+

Violation 1: prod_ids column contains "P1, P2" — a LIST of values, not atomic.
Violation 2: prod_names column contains "Widget, Gadget" — same problem.
Violation 3: qtys column contains "2, 1" — same problem.
Violation 4: prod_ids, prod_names, qtys together form a REPEATING GROUP —
             they are essentially columns {prod1_id, prod1_name, prod1_qty,
             prod2_id, prod2_name, prod2_qty...} collapsed into lists.
```

### The Alternative That Looks Like 1NF But Is Not

Sometimes developers try to fix repeating groups by spreading them across multiple columns instead of collapsing them into lists. This might look like:

```
ORDER_DATA — Repeating Groups Spread Across Columns (still violates 1NF spirit):

+----------+--------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+---------+
| order_id | cust_id| prod1_id| prod1_nm| prod1_qty| prod2_id| prod2_nm| prod2_qty|
+----------+--------+---------+---------+----------+---------+---------+----------+
| 1001     | C01    | P1      | Widget  | 2        | P2      | Gadget  | 1        |
| 1002     | C02    | P1      | Widget  | 5        | NULL    | NULL    | NULL     |
| 1003     | C01    | P3      | Doohi.. | 1        | NULL    | NULL    | NULL     |
| 1004     | C03    | P1      | Widget  | 3        | P3      | Doohi.. | 2        |
+----------+--------+---------+---------+----------+---------+---------+----------+

Problems:
  - What if an order has 3 products? Need prod3_id, prod3_nm, prod3_qty columns.
  - What if an order has 50 products? Impossible.
  - Most rows have NULL for extra columns — wasted storage.
  - Querying "find all orders containing product P1" requires checking every
    prod1_id, prod2_id, prod3_id... column separately.
  - This is a repeating group disguised as separate columns — still violates 1NF.
```

### Achieving 1NF: Flatten the Repeating Groups

The correct fix for both non-atomic values and repeating groups is the same: **move the repeating information into a separate table**, with one row per value. Each order-product combination gets its own row:

```
Step 1: Flatten to 1NF — one row per order-product combination:

ORDER_DATA_1NF:
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+----------+---------+--------+----------+
| order_id | cust_id| cust_name        | cust_email       | zip | prod_id  | prod_nm | qty    | salesman |
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+----------+---------+--------+----------+
| 1001     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| P1       | Widget  | 2      | Bob King |
| 1001     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| P2       | Gadget  | 1      | Bob King |
| 1002     | C02    | Bob Smith        | bob@email.com    |90210| P1       | Widget  | 5      | Alice Ray|
| 1003     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| P3       | Doohi.. | 1      | Bob King |
| 1004     | C03    | Carol White      | carol@email.com  |10001| P1       | Widget  | 3      | Bob King |
| 1004     | C03    | Carol White      | carol@email.com  |10001| P3       | Doohi.. | 2      | Bob King |
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+----------+---------+--------+----------+

Primary Key: (order_id, prod_id) — the combination identifies each row uniquely.
```

The table is now in 1NF. Every cell has exactly one atomic value. There are no lists, no comma-separated values, no repeating groups spread across columns. But look carefully — the redundancy is still enormous. Alice's name and email appear in rows 1001/P1, 1001/P2, and 1003/P3. Carol's information appears in rows 1004/P1 and 1004/P3. The product name "Widget" appears three times. The salesman "Bob King" appears four times. The table satisfies the letter of 1NF but is riddled with redundancy that causes update anomalies. This tells us 1NF was only the beginning.

### What 1NF Fixed and What It Did Not

1NF eliminated the structural impossibility of querying comma-separated cells. You can now ask "give me all orders that contain product P1" with a simple `WHERE prod_id = 'P1'` — no string parsing required. But 1NF did not eliminate redundancy, and it did not prevent the update and deletion anomalies. Alice's email is still stored three times. Bob Smith can still vanish if his only order row is deleted. We need to keep going.

## Chapter 4: Second Normal Form (2NF)

### The Problem of Partial Dependencies

Second Normal Form addresses a specific type of redundancy that arises in tables with **composite primary keys**. Our 1NF table has a composite primary key: `(order_id, prod_id)`. The question 2NF asks is: does every non-key column depend on the **entire** composite key, or does some column depend on only **part** of it?

A **partial dependency** is a functional dependency where a non-key attribute depends on only a proper subset of the composite primary key — not the full key. Partial dependencies are the source of the redundancy we see in our 1NF table.

Let us map out every functional dependency in ORDER_DATA_1NF:

```
Functional Dependencies in ORDER_DATA_1NF:
Primary Key: (order_id, prod_id)

Full-key dependencies (correct — depend on BOTH order_id AND prod_id):
  (order_id, prod_id) → qty              ✓ full dependency

Partial dependencies (PROBLEMS — depend on only PART of the key):
  order_id → cust_id                     ✗ partial (only needs order_id)
  order_id → cust_name                   ✗ partial (only needs order_id)
  order_id → cust_email                  ✗ partial (only needs order_id)
  order_id → zip                         ✗ partial (only needs order_id)
  order_id → salesman                    ✗ partial (only needs order_id)
  prod_id  → prod_name                   ✗ partial (only needs prod_id)
```

Think carefully about what a partial dependency means physically. `order_id → cust_name` means: for every row with the same `order_id`, the `cust_name` will be the same. And since order 1001 has two rows (one for P1 and one for P2), Alice Johnson's name and email are stored twice. This is the mechanical cause of the redundancy we observed: information that depends on only part of the key gets repeated for every row that shares that part.

**A table is in Second Normal Form if it is in 1NF AND there are no partial dependencies.** Every non-key column must depend on the complete primary key, not on any proper subset of it.

Note an important implication: if a table's primary key is a single column (not composite), partial dependencies are impossible by definition — there is no "subset" of a single-column key. Therefore, every 1NF table with a single-column primary key is automatically in 2NF. Partial dependencies can only exist in tables with composite primary keys.

### Curing Partial Dependencies: Decomposition

The cure for partial dependency is always the same: **decompose the table** by moving each partial dependency into its own separate table, taking the determining attribute(s) with it. Each group of attributes that share the same determinant forms a new table, with that determinant as its primary key.

From our analysis, we have three distinct groups:
- Things determined by `order_id` alone: `cust_id`, `cust_name`, `cust_email`, `zip`, `salesman`
- Things determined by `prod_id` alone: `prod_name`
- Things determined by the full `(order_id, prod_id)`: `qty`

But wait — we need to think more carefully. `cust_name`, `cust_email`, and `zip` are not properties of an order; they are properties of a customer. They appear in the order because `order_id → cust_id` and `cust_id → cust_name`. We are mixing two levels of dependency here, which 3NF will address. For now, 2NF just requires us to move these out of the composite-key table.

```
Decomposition to 2NF:

Table 1: ORDERS (things that depend on order_id)
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+-----------+
| order_id | cust_id| cust_name        | cust_email       | zip | salesman  |
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+-----------+
| 1001     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| Bob King  |
| 1002     | C02    | Bob Smith        | bob@email.com    |90210| Alice Ray |
| 1003     | C01    | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  |02101| Bob King  |
| 1004     | C03    | Carol White      | carol@email.com  |10001| Bob King  |
+----------+--------+------------------+------------------+-----+-----------+
Primary Key: order_id

Table 2: PRODUCTS (things that depend on prod_id)
+---------+----------+
| prod_id | prod_name|
+---------+----------+
| P1      | Widget   |
| P2      | Gadget   |
| P3      | Doohi..  |
+---------+----------+
Primary Key: prod_id

Table 3: ORDER_ITEMS (things that depend on BOTH order_id AND prod_id)
+----------+---------+-----+
| order_id | prod_id | qty |
+----------+---------+-----+
| 1001     | P1      | 2   |
| 1001     | P2      | 1   |
| 1002     | P1      | 5   |
| 1003     | P3      | 1   |
| 1004     | P1      | 3   |
| 1004     | P3      | 2   |
+----------+---------+-----+
Primary Key: (order_id, prod_id)
```

Look at what 2NF accomplished. The `PRODUCTS` table now stores "Widget" only once — no matter how many orders contain it. If the name of P1 changes from "Widget" to "Widget Pro," you update exactly one row. The `ORDER_ITEMS` table is clean and compact — one row per line item, nothing extraneous. But the `ORDERS` table still has a problem: Alice Johnson's name, email, and ZIP appear in both rows 1001 and 1003, because she placed two orders. The anomalies affecting customer information persist. This is what 3NF addresses.

## Chapter 5: Third Normal Form (3NF)

### The Problem of Transitive Dependencies

Third Normal Form addresses an even subtler form of redundancy than 2NF. Even after eliminating partial dependencies, a table can still contain redundancy if one non-key column determines another non-key column. This is a **transitive dependency** — one non-key attribute reaches through another non-key attribute to depend on the key.

Look at the ORDERS table we produced from the 2NF step. Its primary key is `order_id`. Let us map its functional dependencies carefully:

```
Functional Dependencies in ORDERS table:
Primary Key: order_id

  order_id → cust_id      ✓ (each order placed by one customer)
  order_id → cust_name    ? (indirectly — via cust_id)
  order_id → cust_email   ? (indirectly — via cust_id)
  order_id → zip          ? (indirectly — via cust_id)
  order_id → salesman     ✓ (each order handled by one salesman)

But also:
  cust_id  → cust_name    ✓ (each customer has one name)
  cust_id  → cust_email   ✓ (each customer has one email)
  cust_id  → zip          ✓ (each customer has one address/zip)

Transitive chain:
  order_id → cust_id → cust_name
  order_id → cust_id → cust_email
  order_id → cust_id → zip

  cust_name, cust_email, and zip depend on order_id ONLY INDIRECTLY —
  they truly belong to cust_id, which happens to be determined by order_id.
```

This is the transitive dependency. `cust_name` does not really depend on the order — it depends on the customer. The order only determines the customer, and the customer determines the name. The dependency chain is: `order → customer → name`. When you store `cust_name` in the `ORDERS` table, you are storing it in the wrong place. It belongs in a `CUSTOMERS` table.

The physical consequence is the same as always: because `cust_id → cust_name`, and because Alice's `cust_id` appears in two orders, her name is stored twice. If she changes her name, you must update both order rows. This is the update anomaly that 3NF eliminates.

**A table is in Third Normal Form if it is in 2NF AND there are no transitive dependencies.** More precisely: every non-key attribute must depend directly and solely on the primary key — not on any other non-key attribute.

There is a famous, memorable formulation of 3NF by Kent (1983): *"Every non-key attribute must depend on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key."* The "whole key" part is 2NF (no partial dependencies). The "nothing but the key" part is 3NF (no transitive dependencies, no dependence on non-key attributes). Together, they give you a table where every column is anchored directly and exclusively to the primary key.

### Identifying Transitive Dependencies Systematically

To check for transitive dependencies in a table, ask this question for every pair of non-key columns A and B: "Does A → B?" If yes — if knowing A tells you B — and if A is not itself the primary key, then B is transitively dependent on the primary key (via A), and B belongs in A's table, not this one.

```
Checking ORDERS for transitive dependencies:

Non-key columns: cust_id, cust_name, cust_email, zip, salesman

Does cust_id → cust_name?  YES → cust_name transitively depends on order_id via cust_id
Does cust_id → cust_email? YES → cust_email transitively depends on order_id via cust_id
Does cust_id → zip?        YES → zip transitively depends on order_id via cust_id
Does cust_id → salesman?   NO  → a salesman is assigned to an order, not a customer
Does cust_name → anything? NO  → names do not determine other non-key attributes
Does zip → city?           YES → but 'city' is not in this table (we removed it)
Does salesman → anything?  NO  → here, salesman is just a name per order

Transitive dependencies found:
  order_id → cust_id → cust_name
  order_id → cust_id → cust_email
  order_id → cust_id → zip

Cure: Move cust_name, cust_email, zip to a CUSTOMERS table keyed by cust_id.
      Leave only cust_id in ORDERS as a foreign key reference.
```

### Curing Transitive Dependencies: Further Decomposition

The cure, as always, is decomposition. Move every transitively-dependent attribute into its own table, keyed by the non-key determinant:

```
Decomposition to 3NF:

Table 1: CUSTOMERS (cust_id → cust_name, cust_email, zip)
+---------+------------------+------------------+-------+
| cust_id | cust_name        | cust_email       | zip   |
+---------+------------------+------------------+-------+
| C01     | Alice Johnson    | alice@email.com  | 02101 |
| C02     | Bob Smith        | bob@email.com    | 90210 |
| C03     | Carol White      | carol@email.com  | 10001 |
+---------+------------------+------------------+-------+
Primary Key: cust_id

Table 2: ORDERS (order_id → cust_id, salesman)
+----------+---------+-----------+
| order_id | cust_id | salesman  |
+----------+---------+-----------+
| 1001     | C01     | Bob King  |
| 1002     | C02     | Alice Ray |
| 1003     | C01     | Bob King  |
| 1004     | C03     | Bob King  |
+----------+---------+-----------+
Primary Key: order_id
Foreign Key: cust_id → customers(cust_id)

Table 3: PRODUCTS (prod_id → prod_name)  [unchanged from 2NF]
+---------+----------+
| prod_id | prod_name|
+---------+----------+
| P1      | Widget   |
| P2      | Gadget   |
| P3      | Doohi..  |
+---------+----------+
Primary Key: prod_id

Table 4: ORDER_ITEMS (order_id, prod_id → qty)  [unchanged from 2NF]
+----------+---------+-----+
| order_id | prod_id | qty |
+----------+---------+-----+
| 1001     | P1      | 2   |
| 1001     | P2      | 1   |
| 1002     | P1      | 5   |
| 1003     | P3      | 1   |
| 1004     | P1      | 3   |
| 1004     | P3      | 2   |
+----------+---------+-----+
Primary Key: (order_id, prod_id)
```

Now examine the `CUSTOMERS` table. Alice Johnson exists exactly once. If her email changes, one row changes, and every order that references her `cust_id` automatically reflects the updated information — because they reference her, rather than copying her data. Bob Smith exists in `CUSTOMERS` even though all his orders could be deleted. Carol White's name appears in one place.

The `ORDERS` table is now clean: one row per order, and the only customer information it stores is the `cust_id` foreign key — a reference, not a copy. There is still a `salesman` column, which we will examine next.

### One More Transitive Dependency: The Salesman

Look at the ORDERS table again. The `salesman` column stores names like "Bob King" and "Alice Ray." This raises a question: does `salesman → anything`? If salespeople have their own IDs, regions, commission rates, or contact information, then yes — `salesman_id → salesman_name`, `salesman_id → region`, and so on. Storing just the salesman's name in `ORDERS` would cause the same transitive dependency problem all over again.

The correct solution is to also extract salespeople into their own table:

```
Recognizing the salesman problem:

  order_id → salesman_id         ✓ (each order handled by one salesperson)
  salesman_id → salesman_name    ✓ (each salesperson has one name)
  salesman_id → region           ✓ (each salesperson covers one region)

  → salesman_name and region are transitively dependent on order_id via salesman_id

Solution:
  SALESPEOPLE table:
  +-------------+---------------+--------+
  | salesman_id | salesman_name | region |
  +-------------+---------------+--------+
  | S01         | Bob King      | East   |
  | S02         | Alice Ray     | West   |
  +-------------+---------------+--------+

  ORDERS table (final 3NF version):
  +----------+---------+-------------+
  | order_id | cust_id | salesman_id |
  +----------+---------+-------------+
  | 1001     | C01     | S01         |
  | 1002     | C02     | S02         |
  | 1003     | C01     | S01         |
  | 1004     | C03     | S01         |
  +----------+---------+-------------+
```

## Chapter 6: The Complete Normalization Journey

### The Full Before-and-After Picture

Let us place the complete transformation side by side so the journey is visible in full:

```
BEFORE (Unnormalized ORDER_DATA — one monstrous table):

  1 table
  9 columns (including multi-valued columns with lists)
  All three anomalies present
  Impossible to store a customer without an order
  Deleting an order can silently erase a customer
  Updating Alice's email requires finding every row she appears in

  Redundancy: STILL SEVERE

  Salesperson info stored once: FIXED
  All three anomalies: ELIMINATED

                                                  └────────────┘
```

### The Three Normal Forms as Three Questions

Looking back at the journey, each normal form is really just one precise question asked of your table:

```
1NF asks: "Are all values atomic, and are there no repeating groups?"
          If any column holds a list, set, or array of values → not 1NF.
          Fix: one row per value; move repeating groups to a new table.

2NF asks: "Does every non-key column depend on the WHOLE primary key?"
          If any non-key column depends on only PART of a composite key → not 2NF.
          Fix: move each partial dependency to its own table, keyed by its determinant.
          (If the primary key is single-column, 2NF is automatic.)

3NF asks: "Does every non-key column depend DIRECTLY on the primary key?"
          If any non-key column A determines another non-key column B → not 3NF.
          Fix: move B to a table keyed by A; leave only A (as a FK) in the current table.
```

## Chapter 7: Anomaly Verification — Did We Actually Fix Everything?

### The Acid Test

The best way to verify that normalization succeeded is to try to reproduce the original anomalies against the new schema and confirm that they are impossible.

**Update Anomaly — ELIMINATED.** Alice Johnson's name and email now exist in exactly one row: `CUSTOMERS` where `cust_id = 'C01'`. To update her email, you change one row. Every order that references her `cust_id` will automatically retrieve the updated email the next time you query it. There is no risk of inconsistency because the data exists in only one place.

**Insertion Anomaly — ELIMINATED.** A new customer, Dave, can be inserted into the `CUSTOMERS` table without any order existing. `CUSTOMERS` is an independent table. Dave's existence does not require an `ORDER` to accompany it. The foreign key constraint runs from `ORDERS` to `CUSTOMERS` — orders reference customers, not the other way around. Creating a customer with no orders is completely valid.

**Deletion Anomaly — ELIMINATED.** Deleting Bob Smith's only order (order 1002) removes one row from `ORDERS` and one row from `ORDER_ITEMS`. The `CUSTOMERS` table is completely untouched. Bob Smith still exists as a customer; his name, email, and ZIP remain intact. The information about the cancelled order and the information about the customer's existence are now stored in separate tables, so deleting one cannot accidentally destroy the other.

## Chapter 8: Denormalization — The Intentional Retreat

### When Perfect Normalization Works Against You

Normalization is the path toward correctness and integrity. But it is not always the path toward performance. In a fully normalized schema, reconstructing a complete picture of even a simple fact — "show me order 1001 with the customer name and all the ordered products with their names" — requires joining five tables: `ORDERS`, `CUSTOMERS`, `ORDER_ITEMS`, `PRODUCTS`, and `SALESPEOPLE`.

For an OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) system — one that processes individual transactions, inserts, and updates — 3NF is almost always the right choice. The integrity guarantees outweigh the JOIN cost, and individual queries touch small amounts of data.

But for an OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) system — one that runs large analytical queries over millions of rows — JOINs across many tables on huge datasets can become a bottleneck. Data warehouses and analytical systems often deliberately **denormalize** their data: they re-introduce some redundancy to reduce the number of JOINs required for their most common queries.

```
OLTP System (normalized — many small reads/writes, high integrity needed):
  CUSTOMERS, ORDERS, PRODUCTS, ORDER_ITEMS, SALESPEOPLE
  → 5 tables, JOINs required, but writes are fast and consistent

OLAP System (denormalized — few large analytical reads, writes are batch):
  FACT_ORDER_ITEMS: one row per order-item with ALL attributes
    (order_id, order_date, cust_id, cust_name, cust_region,
     prod_id, prod_name, prod_category, qty, unit_price,
     salesman_id, salesman_name, salesman_region, ...)
  → 1 wide table, no JOINs, fast analytical scans, but redundant
```

Denormalization is a conscious, deliberate decision made for specific performance reasons in specific contexts. It is NOT an excuse for sloppy design. You denormalize because you understand the trade-off, you accept the loss of some integrity guarantees, and you compensate with application-level controls or ETL processes that keep the denormalized data consistent.

The rule of thumb is: **normalize first, always. Denormalize only when you have measured a performance problem and determined that reducing JOINs is the solution.** Premature denormalization is one of the most common and costly mistakes in database design.

## Epilogue: Normalization as Listening to Your Data

There is a deeper way to think about normalization beyond the rules and the anomalies. When you normalize a database, you are listening to what the data is telling you about the real world.

When you discover that customer information repeats across order rows, the data is telling you: *"Customers and orders are separate things. They have their own independent existence. A customer can exist without an order. An order references a customer; it does not contain one."*

When you discover that product names depend only on `prod_id` and not on `order_id`, the data is telling you: *"A product is a product, regardless of which orders contain it. The product's identity is independent of any order."*

When you discover that a ZIP code determines a city, and a city is stored alongside ZIP in the customer record, the data is telling you: *"ZIP codes and cities have their own relationship, independent of customers. If you store them together, you are tangling two facts that do not belong together."*

Each normal form is a formalization of the intuition that **each piece of information should live in exactly the place that owns it** — the table whose key is the most direct, meaningful determinant of that information. A customer's name is owned by the customer. A product's price is owned by the product. A grade is owned by the enrollment (the relationship between a student and a course).

When every piece of information is stored in the place that owns it — and only there — the database mirrors the structure of reality. Updates are simple because reality has only one version of each fact. The database should too.
