# The Deletion That Took Six Hours Every Night: How Table Partitioning Turned a Nightly I/O Crisis into a Three-Second DROP

> _"Deleting a billion rows is one of the most expensive things you can ask a relational database to do. Every row must be found, locked, marked dead, written to the WAL, and eventually cleaned up by vacuum. Dropping a partition table is one of the cheapest — it is a single metadata operation. The difference between these two approaches is not a matter of degree. It is a matter of design."_

---

## Setting the Scene

You are a database engineer at a financial technology company. Like all companies operating in regulated industries, the company is required by law to retain a complete audit log of every significant action taken on customer accounts — logins, password changes, fund transfers, account modifications, permission grants, and dozens of other event types. Each action generates one or more rows in the `audit_log` table.

Compliance also dictates the retention period: audit records must be kept for a minimum of ninety days and may be deleted thereafter. The company implemented this retention policy as a nightly scheduled job — a cron task that runs at 2:00 AM every night and deletes all audit records older than ninety days:

```sql
DELETE FROM audit_log
WHERE created_at < NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days';
```

When the system was first deployed three years ago, the audit log had perhaps ten million rows, and this nightly deletion finished in about ninety seconds. Acceptable. Nobody thought much about it.

Three years later, the audit log has grown to two billion rows. The platform has hundreds of thousands of active users performing dozens of auditable actions per day. The nightly deletion job is no longer finishing in ninety seconds. It is running for six full hours — from 2:00 AM to 8:00 AM. During those six hours, the database server is under severe I/O stress: disk write throughput is saturated from WAL writes, autovacuum is struggling to process the dead tuples left behind, replication lag spikes to several minutes, and application response times degrade noticeably for early-rising users.

The operations team has started receiving pager alerts at 4:00 AM when replication lag crosses the threshold. The engineering manager calls an emergency meeting. The team needs to fix the nightly retention job without disrupting the compliance requirement and without taking the system offline for a multi-day migration.

Your senior database engineer takes the call. She says: _"The problem is not that we are deleting too many rows. The problem is that we are deleting rows at all. We need to stop deleting rows and start dropping tables."_

## Understanding Why Bulk DELETE is Catastrophically Expensive

To appreciate how partitioning solves this problem, you first need to understand precisely why deleting a large number of rows from a large table is so expensive — not just slow, but actively harmful to the entire database system while it is running.

When you execute `DELETE FROM audit_log WHERE created_at < NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days'`, the database does not simply remove the matching rows and compact the table. PostgreSQL uses a storage model called **MVCC — Multi-Version Concurrency Control** — which never physically removes data immediately. Instead, it marks matching rows as "dead" by updating their visibility information in the row header. The actual physical space occupied by those rows is not reclaimed at delete time — it is left in place, marked as dead, invisible to new transactions but still occupying storage on disk.

The reclamation of this dead space is the job of **autovacuum** — PostgreSQL's background process that periodically scans tables, identifies dead tuples, and cleans them up so their storage can be reused. A DELETE that removes hundreds of millions of rows in one transaction creates an enormous backlog of dead tuples that autovacuum must then process. Autovacuum runs aggressively after a large DELETE, consuming CPU and I/O resources for hours or days after the DELETE completes. During this autovacuum processing, the table is less efficient — its pages are full of dead tuples that take up space and must be skipped during scans.

On top of the MVCC overhead, every deleted row must be recorded in the **Write-Ahead Log (WAL)**. A deletion of one hundred million rows generates an enormous WAL volume — potentially hundreds of gigabytes — that must be flushed to disk for durability and streamed to read replicas. This WAL flood is what causes the replication lag spike: replicas receive hundreds of gigabytes of WAL and must apply all of it before they are caught up with the primary.

```
WHY BULK DELETE CAUSES A SIX-HOUR CRISIS

DELETE FROM audit_log WHERE created_at < NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days'
Affects: ~200,000,000 rows (the oldest 90 days of a 2B-row table
         gets replaced daily — roughly 200M rows expire per cycle)

Phase 1: Deletion (2-3 hours)
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  For each of 200M rows:                                     │
  │    - Lock the row                                           │
  │    - Write "dead" marker to row header                      │
  │    - Write DELETE record to WAL                             │
  │  WAL generated: ~40-80 GB (row headers + delete records)   │
  │  Replication lag: growing from 0 to several minutes        │
  │  Disk write I/O: saturated                                 │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Phase 2: Autovacuum aftermath (3-6 hours after DELETE)
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Autovacuum must scan all table pages for dead tuples       │
  │  200M dead tuples scattered across thousands of pages       │
  │  Must read each page, identify dead tuples, reclaim space   │
  │  Writes free space map updates, visibility map updates      │
  │  Additional WAL generated by vacuum: tens of GB            │
  │  CPU and I/O load: high, sustained for hours               │
  │  Table bloat during vacuum: 200M dead rows taking space     │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Phase 3: Application impact
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Early users (7-8 AM) experience slow responses             │
  │  Read replicas lagging → some reads fall to primary         │
  │  Primary under autovacuum load → query latency increases    │
  │  Disk I/O shared between vacuum and application queries     │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

This six-hour disruption happens every night. Every night, two hundred million rows expire, are laboriously deleted through the MVCC mechanism, and the aftermath takes the rest of the night to clean up. By the time autovacuum finishes, it is nearly time for the next deletion cycle to begin.

The root cause is not the volume of data being deleted — it is the mechanism. Deleting rows one by one through the MVCC system was designed for removing small numbers of rows from live transactional tables, not for bulk time-based data expiry at the scale of hundreds of millions of rows per night.

## The Insight: Data Expiry is a Table-Level Operation

The fundamental insight that makes partitioning the correct solution is this: the ninety-day retention policy does not say "delete specific rows based on their content." It says "all data older than ninety days should be removed." This is not a row-level operation — it is a time-range operation. An entire slice of time — everything from three months ago and earlier — needs to be removed.

When you think of the audit log as a time-ordered sequence of data, the retention policy says: "periodically, the oldest segment of this sequence expires entirely." Not some rows from that segment, not rows matching a complex condition within that segment — the entire segment, uniformly, completely.

A table, in PostgreSQL, is a unit of physical storage that can be created or destroyed as an atomic metadata operation. Creating a table is instantaneous — the database creates a few entries in its system catalog and allocates a file on disk. Dropping a table is equally instantaneous — the system catalog entries are removed and the file is scheduled for deletion. No row-by-row processing, no MVCC markers, no WAL records for individual rows, no autovacuum cleanup needed.

If each time-segment of the audit log were its own table, expiring a segment would be a single `DROP TABLE` — a metadata operation completing in milliseconds. This is exactly what **table partitioning** provides.

```
THE CORE INSIGHT: EXPIRY IS A TABLE-LEVEL OPERATION

  Audit log as a single table (current state):
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  audit_log table                                            │
  │  ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐   │
  │  │ Aug 23 │ │ Sep 23 │ │ Oct 23 │ │ Nov 23 │ │ ... 24 │   │
  │  │ rows   │ │ rows   │ │ rows   │ │ rows   │ │ rows   │   │
  │  └────────┘ └────────┘ └────────┘ └────────┘ └────────┘   │
  │                ↑ scattered throughout the same table file   │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  To expire Aug 2023: DELETE 200M rows scattered across the file
  Cost: hours of MVCC marking, WAL writes, vacuum cleanup

  Audit log as monthly partitions (after migration):
  ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐
  │aug_2023│ │sep_2023│ │oct_2023│ │nov_2023│ │dec_2023│ ...
  │ table  │ │ table  │ │ table  │ │ table  │ │ table  │
  └────────┘ └────────┘ └────────┘ └────────┘ └────────┘
                ↑ each partition is a physically separate file
  To expire Aug 2023: DROP TABLE audit_log_2023_08
  Cost: one metadata operation, milliseconds, no vacuum needed
```

## How PostgreSQL Range Partitioning Works

PostgreSQL has supported **declarative table partitioning** since version 10, with significant improvements in versions 11, 12, and beyond. Range partitioning allows you to define a parent table that is divided into child partitions, each covering a non-overlapping range of values in the partition key column. When a row is inserted into the parent table, PostgreSQL automatically routes it to the correct child partition based on its partition key value. When a query reads from the parent table, PostgreSQL automatically reads only from the partitions whose ranges overlap with the query's filter conditions — this optimisation is called **partition pruning**.

The partitioned audit log schema looks like this:

```sql
-- Parent table: defines the structure and partition strategy
CREATE TABLE audit_log (
    id              BIGSERIAL,
    event_type      TEXT         NOT NULL,
    created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ  NOT NULL,
    user_id         BIGINT       NOT NULL,
    resource_type   TEXT         NOT NULL,
    resource_id     BIGINT,
    action          TEXT         NOT NULL,
    metadata        JSONB,
    ip_address      INET,
    session_id      TEXT,

    PRIMARY KEY (id, created_at)   -- partition key must be in PK
) PARTITION BY RANGE (created_at);

-- Child partitions: one per month
CREATE TABLE audit_log_2024_01
    PARTITION OF audit_log
    FOR VALUES FROM ('2024-01-01') TO ('2024-02-01');

CREATE TABLE audit_log_2024_02
    PARTITION OF audit_log
    FOR VALUES FROM ('2024-02-01') TO ('2024-03-01');

CREATE TABLE audit_log_2024_03
    PARTITION OF audit_log
    FOR VALUES FROM ('2024-03-01') TO ('2024-04-01');

-- ... and so on for each month

-- Each partition can have its own indexes
CREATE INDEX ON audit_log_2024_01 (user_id, created_at);
CREATE INDEX ON audit_log_2024_02 (user_id, created_at);
-- (or inherited from parent in newer PostgreSQL versions)
```

Each `PARTITION OF` definition creates a physically separate table on disk — its own file, its own storage, its own indexes. These child tables are owned by the parent and inherit its schema, but their data is stored independently. The parent table itself contains no rows — it is purely a routing and query interface.

```
PARTITION ROUTING ON INSERT

  INSERT INTO audit_log (event_type, created_at, user_id, ...)
  VALUES ('login', '2024-03-15 14:23:01', 42, ...)

  PostgreSQL checks partition key (created_at = '2024-03-15'):
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ audit_log_2024_01: FROM '2024-01-01' TO '2024-02-01' ✗ │
  │ audit_log_2024_02: FROM '2024-02-01' TO '2024-03-01' ✗ │
  │ audit_log_2024_03: FROM '2024-03-01' TO '2024-04-01' ✓ │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  Row automatically routed to: audit_log_2024_03
  Application code: unchanged — still inserts into audit_log
```

From the application's perspective, nothing changes. The application inserts into `audit_log` and queries from `audit_log` exactly as before. The partitioning is entirely transparent — the database handles the routing and the pruning invisibly.

## The Retention Job After Partitioning

With monthly partitions in place, the nightly retention job transforms from a multi-hour ordeal into a seconds-long administrative operation. Instead of deleting rows, it drops the partition tables that are now older than ninety days:

```sql
-- Old retention job (6 hours, catastrophic):
DELETE FROM audit_log
WHERE created_at < NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days';

-- New retention job (3 seconds, trivial):
-- (Run on the 1st of each month, or nightly with a check)

DO $$
DECLARE
    partition_name TEXT;
    cutoff_month   DATE := DATE_TRUNC('month', NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days');
BEGIN
    -- Find and drop all partitions older than 90 days
    FOR partition_name IN
        SELECT child.relname
        FROM pg_inherits
        JOIN pg_class parent ON pg_inherits.inhparent = parent.oid
        JOIN pg_class child  ON pg_inherits.inhrelid  = child.oid
        WHERE parent.relname = 'audit_log'
          AND child.relname < 'audit_log_' ||
              TO_CHAR(cutoff_month, 'YYYY_MM')
    LOOP
        EXECUTE 'DROP TABLE ' || quote_ident(partition_name);
        RAISE NOTICE 'Dropped partition: %', partition_name;
    END LOOP;
END $$;
```

The `DROP TABLE` operation on a partition is a **metadata-only operation**. PostgreSQL removes the partition's entry from the system catalog, unlinks the physical file, and the operating system schedules the file for deletion. No rows are visited. No MVCC markers are written. No WAL records are generated for individual rows — only a tiny WAL record for the catalog change. No autovacuum work is required. No I/O spike occurs. The operation completes in milliseconds, regardless of whether the partition contained one million rows or one billion.

```
DROP TABLE vs DELETE — What the database actually does

DELETE FROM audit_log WHERE created_at < cutoff:
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  1. Full scan of all pages in audit_log looking for old rows│
  │  2. For each matching row: write MVCC dead marker           │
  │  3. For each matching row: write DELETE record to WAL       │
  │  4. Flush WAL to disk continuously (I/O intensive)         │
  │  5. After commit: autovacuum starts processing dead tuples  │
  │  6. Autovacuum scans pages, reclaims space, updates maps   │
  │  Rows processed: 200,000,000                               │
  │  WAL generated: ~40-80 GB                                  │
  │  Time: 6 hours                                             │
  │  Autovacuum aftermath: additional hours                    │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

DROP TABLE audit_log_2023_08:
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  1. Remove entry from pg_class (system catalog)            │
  │  2. Write one catalog change record to WAL                 │
  │  3. Schedule data file for OS-level deletion               │
  │  OS deletes the file: instantaneous from DB perspective    │
  │  Rows processed: 0                                         │
  │  WAL generated: ~1 KB (catalog change only)               │
  │  Time: ~3 seconds (including pg_inherits lookup)           │
  │  Autovacuum aftermath: none needed                        │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  The difference is not optimisation. It is a different operation entirely.
```

## Partition Pruning: The Query Performance Bonus

The retention improvement is the headline benefit, but table partitioning brings a significant additional benefit to query performance through **partition pruning**. When a query includes a filter on the partition key column, PostgreSQL's planner can determine at planning time which partitions could possibly contain matching rows and exclude all others from the query plan entirely.

For example, a query asking for all login events in the last thirty days:

```sql
SELECT * FROM audit_log
WHERE event_type = 'login'
  AND created_at > NOW() - INTERVAL '30 days';
```

On the non-partitioned two-billion-row table, this query must either perform a full table scan (if there is no index on `created_at`) or an index range scan that touches approximately sixty-seven million rows (thirty days of events across a two-billion-row, three-year table). The planner must consider the entire table.

On the partitioned table, the planner sees that `created_at > NOW() - INTERVAL '30 days'` means only the current month's partition and possibly last month's partition can contain relevant rows. It prunes all other partitions from the query plan entirely — they are not even opened or scanned. The query operates only on two partitions containing approximately sixty-seven million rows combined, ignoring the other twenty-two partitions containing the remaining 1.93 billion rows.

```
PARTITION PRUNING IN ACTION

  Query: WHERE created_at > NOW() - INTERVAL '30 days'
  Current date: 2024-09-13

  Partitions considered (without pruning):
  audit_log_2021_10  ← 2 years old    → scanned (wasted)
  audit_log_2021_11  ← 2 years old    → scanned (wasted)
  ... (22 months of wasted scanning)
  audit_log_2024_08  ← last month     → scanned (needed)
  audit_log_2024_09  ← this month     → scanned (needed)

  Partitions considered (with partition pruning):
  audit_log_2021_10  ← range ends before 30-day cutoff → PRUNED ✗
  audit_log_2021_11  ← range ends before 30-day cutoff → PRUNED ✗
  ... (22 months automatically excluded)
  audit_log_2024_08  ← range overlaps with 30-day window → SCAN ✓
  audit_log_2024_09  ← range overlaps with 30-day window → SCAN ✓

  Pages read: 2 partitions instead of 24
  Rows considered: ~67M instead of 2,000M
  Speedup from pruning alone: ~10-15×
```

This partition pruning benefit is automatic — no query changes are required. Any query that filters on `created_at` (the partition key) will benefit from pruning. Audit queries asking "show all events for user X in the last week," compliance queries asking "show all failed logins in the past month," and operational queries asking "show all events from yesterday" will all execute dramatically faster because the planner eliminates irrelevant partitions before the query begins.

## The Live Migration Strategy: Moving 2 Billion Rows Without Downtime

Converting an existing two-billion-row unpartitioned table to a partitioned one is a significant engineering challenge. You cannot simply add `PARTITION BY` to an existing table — PostgreSQL requires you to create a new partitioned table and migrate the data. Doing this on a live production system, without downtime, requires a carefully sequenced plan.

The strategy used in this case study follows five phases:

**Phase 1: Create the new partitioned table structure.** Define the partitioned parent table and all the partition child tables for the time range covering the data you need to migrate. This is purely schema work — no data is touched.

```sql
-- Create new partitioned table alongside existing one
CREATE TABLE audit_log_new (
    -- same columns as audit_log --
) PARTITION BY RANGE (created_at);

-- Create all needed partitions
CREATE TABLE audit_log_new_2021_10 PARTITION OF audit_log_new
    FOR VALUES FROM ('2021-10-01') TO ('2021-11-01');
-- ... (create all partitions from earliest data to future)
```

**Phase 2: Migrate historical data in batches.** Copy data from the old table to the new partitioned table, working backwards from the oldest data first (since that data is immutable and will not change during migration). Process one month at a time to limit transaction size and avoid excessive I/O in any one batch.

```sql
-- Copy one month at a time (run this in a loop for each month)
INSERT INTO audit_log_new
SELECT * FROM audit_log
WHERE created_at >= '2021-10-01'
  AND created_at <  '2021-11-01';
```

**Phase 3: Dual-write bridge.** Once historical data is copied, configure the application or use a trigger on the old table to write new records to both the old table and the new partitioned table simultaneously. This ensures no data is missed during the transition window.

**Phase 4: Atomic cutover.** In a brief maintenance window (or using `pg_reorg`/locking strategies for zero-downtime), rename the old table away and rename the new partitioned table to take its place. All application connections immediately begin reading from and writing to the new partitioned table.

**Phase 5: Drop the old table.** After verifying the new table is working correctly and the row counts match, drop the old unpartitioned table. The massive disk space is reclaimed.

```
MIGRATION TIMELINE

  Week 1: Create partitioned table structure (minutes)
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Week 1-4: Batch migrate historical data
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  Night 1: Copy Oct 2021 → ~70M rows, ~4 hours           │
  │  Night 2: Copy Nov 2021 → ~70M rows, ~4 hours           │
  │  Night 3: Copy Dec 2021 → ~70M rows, ~4 hours           │
  │  ...                                                     │
  │  (One month per night, running at low-traffic hours)     │
  │  Application: still writing to old table                 │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  Week 5: Enable dual-write trigger (minutes)
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Week 6: Final sync of any recent data, cutover (minutes)
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Week 6+: Drop old table, begin dropping expired partitions
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Total migration: ~4-6 weeks, no downtime, no service disruption
```

An alternative approach for truly zero-downtime migration uses the `pg_partman` extension, which provides tooling for converting unpartitioned tables to partitioned ones using a "swap" method, and automates partition creation and retention management going forward.

## Automating Future Partition Creation and Retention

After the migration, two ongoing maintenance tasks must be automated to prevent the system from requiring manual intervention indefinitely: creating new partition tables before they are needed, and dropping old partition tables when they expire.

The `pg_partman` extension is the standard PostgreSQL tool for this. Once configured, it can automatically create new monthly partitions in advance (so that inserts never fail due to a missing partition) and drop or detach old partitions according to a retention policy.

Without `pg_partman`, the same logic can be implemented with a cron job or a database scheduler:

```sql
-- Create next month's partition (run monthly, on the 20th of each month)
DO $$
DECLARE
    next_month      DATE := DATE_TRUNC('month', NOW() + INTERVAL '1 month');
    next_month_end  DATE := DATE_TRUNC('month', NOW() + INTERVAL '2 months');
    partition_name  TEXT := 'audit_log_' ||
                            TO_CHAR(next_month, 'YYYY_MM');
BEGIN
    EXECUTE FORMAT(
        'CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS %I
         PARTITION OF audit_log
         FOR VALUES FROM (%L) TO (%L)',
        partition_name, next_month, next_month_end
    );
    RAISE NOTICE 'Created partition: %', partition_name;
END $$;
```

```
PARTITION LIFECYCLE MANAGEMENT

  Monthly timeline:
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  1st of each month:                                         │
  │    Retention job: DROP partitions older than 90 days        │
  │    (typically drops 1-2 partitions that newly expired)      │
  │    Duration: 3 seconds                                      │
  │                                                             │
  │  20th of each month:                                        │
  │    Partition creation job: CREATE next month's partition     │
  │    (ensures partition exists before month begins)           │
  │    Duration: < 1 second                                     │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  What the partition timeline looks like at any given time:
  (assuming current date is 2024-09-13 and 90-day retention)

  audit_log_2024_06  ← 90 days ago: expires soon, kept for now
  audit_log_2024_07  ← active, will expire in ~3 weeks
  audit_log_2024_08  ← active, recent
  audit_log_2024_09  ← active, current month (receiving inserts)
  audit_log_2024_10  ← future, pre-created and ready
  audit_log_2024_11  ← future, will be created on Oct 20th

  Partitions in existence: ~6 at any given time (3 months + buffer)
  vs. before: 1 monolithic table growing forever
```

## One More Tool: DETACH PARTITION for Archival

A variation on the DROP approach is worth mentioning for cases where you do not want to permanently destroy the data but instead want to archive it. PostgreSQL supports detaching a partition from its parent table while keeping the child table in place:

```sql
-- Detach partition instead of dropping it (for archival)
ALTER TABLE audit_log
DETACH PARTITION audit_log_2024_06;

-- audit_log_2024_06 is now a standalone table
-- It can be:
-- 1. Dumped to a file: pg_dump -t audit_log_2024_06 > archive.sql
-- 2. Moved to a cheaper tablespace: ALTER TABLE ... SET TABLESPACE archive_ts
-- 3. Compressed and stored offline
-- 4. Queried independently: SELECT * FROM audit_log_2024_06
-- 5. Dropped when archival is confirmed: DROP TABLE audit_log_2024_06

-- Detaching is also instantaneous — metadata operation only
```

`DETACH PARTITION` is instantaneous for the same reason `DROP TABLE` is — it is a metadata operation, not a data movement operation. The detached partition becomes a regular standalone table that the parent table no longer knows about. It can be archived, compressed, moved to cheaper storage, or eventually dropped. This is the correct pattern for compliance scenarios where records must be retained in an accessible but offline form for several years after the ninety-day active retention window expires.

## The Broader Pattern: Time-Based Data and Partition-Based Retention

The audit log case is not unique. The same pattern applies to any table that exhibits these three characteristics simultaneously: it grows continuously over time as new records are appended; it has a time-based retention policy that expires all records beyond a certain age; and the expired records can be identified purely by their timestamp, not by any more complex condition.

This pattern appears in logging tables, event streams, metric time series, session records, telemetry data, financial transaction archives, and many other common use cases. Any time you find yourself writing a nightly or weekly `DELETE FROM large_table WHERE created_at < cutoff`, you should ask whether time-based partitioning is the appropriate design for that table.

The design principle is sometimes stated as: **if you will ever need to delete data by time range, partition by time from day one.** Retrofitting partitioning onto an existing large table is possible — as this case study demonstrates — but it requires a multi-week migration and careful planning. Designing for partitioning from the start costs almost nothing extra and prevents the migration effort entirely.

## The Lesson That Stays With You

The nightly retention job that took six hours is, in retrospect, a perfectly predictable outcome of a misaligned tool choice. `DELETE` is the right tool for removing specific rows that no longer belong in a table based on business logic — cancelling a specific order, removing a specific user's data on GDPR request, correcting an erroneous record. It is not the right tool for bulk time-based data expiry.

For time-based data expiry, the right tool is partition management — specifically, `DROP TABLE` on a time-bounded partition. The operation that `DELETE` expresses in a complicated, expensive, row-by-row way is expressed by `DROP TABLE` in a single metadata operation. The key is that partitioning must be designed in from the start — or migrated to — for this approach to be available.

The lesson is architectural: before you write a large table, ask what will happen to its oldest data over time. If the answer is "it will be deleted on a schedule," the follow-up question is "could each scheduled deletion be expressed as dropping a partition?" If yes, design the table with partitioning from the beginning. The cost of designing for partitioning upfront is negligible. The cost of not doing so — six-hour nightly I/O crises, multi-week migrations, replication lag, pager alerts at 4 AM — accumulates over years and can become one of the most expensive technical debts in the infrastructure.

A well-designed partitioned table does not just handle today's data. It handles three years of data, five years of data, ten years of data — with the same constant-time retention operations, regardless of how much history accumulates. That is the promise of partition-based retention: it does not degrade with scale. It stays at three seconds, whether the table has two billion rows or twenty billion.

## Summary

This case study follows a two-billion-row audit log table whose nightly retention job — `DELETE FROM audit_log WHERE created_at < NOW() - INTERVAL '90 days'` — was taking six hours and causing severe database stress. The `DELETE` operation created two hundred million MVCC dead tuples per night, generating forty to eighty gigabytes of WAL, causing replication lag spikes of four to eight minutes, and triggering hours of autovacuum cleanup that degraded application performance through the early morning hours.

The solution was to migrate the monolithic table to a declarative monthly range partition scheme, with one physical child table per calendar month. The retention job was rewritten to execute `DROP TABLE` on the expired monthly partition, a metadata-only operation that completes in three seconds regardless of partition size. No rows are visited, no MVCC markers written, no WAL flood generated, no autovacuum work required. As a secondary benefit, partition pruning allows queries filtered by time to execute against only the relevant partitions, reducing query execution times by ten to fifteen times for time-bounded audit searches.

The migration was performed live over several weeks using a phased approach: creating the partitioned schema, migrating historical data month by month in overnight batches, enabling dual-write, performing an atomic cutover, and dropping the old table. The retention job duration went from 21,600 seconds to 3 seconds — a 7,200-fold improvement — and the nightly I/O crisis was permanently eliminated.

The lesson is foundational: `DELETE` is designed for removing specific rows; `DROP TABLE` on a partition is designed for time-based data expiry. Partitioned tables allow data retention to be expressed as a table lifecycle operation rather than a row scanning operation. If your data has a time-based retention policy, partition it by time. If you are designing a new high-volume append table, ask at design time whether time-based partitioning should be part of the schema — the cost of adding it upfront is negligible; the cost of retrofitting it under pressure is substantial.
