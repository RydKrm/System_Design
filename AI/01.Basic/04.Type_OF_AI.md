# Types of Artificial Intelligence: A Guide for the Newly Joined Engineer

_A continuation of the foundation guide — this time stepping back from how models are built to classify AI systems along two different axes: how capable they are, and how they relate to memory and the world around them._

---

## Two Different Ways of Classifying AI

Before walking through the six terms on your list, it is worth pausing on something that confuses almost every engineer the first time they encounter this topic: the six categories you have been given do not all belong to the same classification scheme. The first three terms, Narrow AI, General AI, and Superintelligence, classify AI by capability, essentially asking the question "how broadly and how well can this system think?" The next three terms, Reactive Machines, Limited Memory AI, and Generative AI Systems, classify AI by functionality, asking instead "how does this system relate to memory, context, and the world it operates in?" These two schemes are not competing definitions of the same thing; they are two independent lenses looking at the same underlying landscape of systems, the way you might classify a database both by its consistency model and, separately, by whether it is relational or document-oriented. Keeping these two axes mentally separate from the very start will save you from a great deal of confusion later.

```
                         TWO INDEPENDENT AXES FOR CLASSIFYING AI

   AXIS 1: BY CAPABILITY                        AXIS 2: BY FUNCTIONALITY
   (how broad and how smart?)                   (how does it use memory and context?)

   Narrow AI  --->  General AI  --->  Super-    Reactive Machines  --->  Limited Memory
   (ANI)            (AGI)             intelligence                      AI  --->  Generative
                                       (ASI)                                       AI Systems
```

## Narrow AI (ANI)

Narrow AI, often abbreviated as ANI for Artificial Narrow Intelligence, refers to any AI system that is designed and trained to perform one specific task, or a tightly related cluster of tasks, and nothing beyond that scope. It is important for you to understand, as a practicing engineer, that every single AI system that exists and operates in the real world today, without exception, is Narrow AI; this includes the spam filter sorting your inbox, the recommendation engine suggesting what to watch next, the fraud detection system scanning transactions, the computer vision model inspecting products on a factory line, and even the most advanced large language models you interact with daily, because, as impressively general their conversational abilities may feel, they remain fundamentally systems trained to predict the next piece of text, operating within a defined scope rather than possessing genuine, human-like understanding across every conceivable domain of existence.

The defining characteristic of Narrow AI, the thing that should immediately identify a system as narrow, is that its competence does not transfer; a model trained with extraordinary skill at playing the game of chess has no inherent ability whatsoever to drive a car or diagnose a disease, even though both tasks involve pattern recognition and decision-making, because the knowledge it acquired through training is deeply specific to the narrow task and the narrow data it was exposed to. As an engineer, this is actually liberating news rather than discouraging news, because it means that every AI system you will ever be asked to design, deploy, or integrate into a backend service is, by definition, solving one well-defined problem, and your job is simply to identify that problem precisely, choose the right narrow tool for it, and build solid, reliable infrastructure around it, in much the same spirit as choosing Redis for caching and RabbitMQ for messaging rather than expecting one single technology to handle every responsibility in your system.

```
         NARROW AI (ANI) — everything that exists today
   +---------------------------------------------------------+
   |  Chess Engine     Spam Filter     Fraud Detector         |
   |  Image Classifier     Recommendation System              |
   |  Large Language Model (fluent, but still task-bound)     |
   +---------------------------------------------------------+
        Each system excels at its own task and does not
        transfer that competence to unrelated tasks.
```

## General AI (AGI)

General AI, or Artificial General Intelligence, abbreviated AGI, describes a hypothetical future system that would possess the ability to understand, learn, and apply knowledge and reasoning across any intellectual task that a human being can perform, matching or exceeding human-level flexibility rather than being confined to one narrow domain. The defining property that would separate AGI from everything described in the Narrow AI section above is precisely the transfer of competence that Narrow AI lacks; an AGI system, upon learning to play chess, would be expected to apply the same underlying reasoning faculties to, say, learning to cook a new recipe or negotiate a business contract, without needing to be separately engineered and trained from scratch for each new domain, in the same general way a human who becomes skilled at one intellectual pursuit can usually apply portions of that reasoning ability to entirely unrelated pursuits.

As of the writing of this guide, Artificial General Intelligence does not exist anywhere in the world, and there is genuine, ongoing, and often heated disagreement among serious researchers in the field about how far away such a system might be, whether current approaches such as scaling up Transformer-based language models will eventually lead to it at all, or whether entirely new architectural breakthroughs, still undiscovered, will be required. It is worth being direct with you about this because the term AGI is used extremely loosely in popular media and marketing material, where it is sometimes applied to systems that are, in reality, still clearly Narrow AI exhibiting impressively broad but ultimately bounded competence; as an engineer entering this field, you should treat any claim of "general intelligence" in a commercial product with healthy, informed skepticism, and reserve the term for the specific, still-unrealized capability described here.

## Superintelligence (ASI) — theoretical concept

Superintelligence, abbreviated ASI for Artificial Superintelligence, describes a further, even more speculative hypothetical stage beyond AGI, in which a system's intellectual capability would not merely match human ability across all domains, as AGI would, but would substantially and comprehensively exceed the best human performance in essentially every field of endeavor, including scientific research, creative work, strategic planning, and social reasoning. The concept is explicitly theoretical and exists today purely in the realm of philosophical and academic discussion, research papers exploring long-term safety considerations, and speculative fiction, rather than in any engineering roadmap with concrete technical milestones; no credible, peer-reviewed technical pathway currently exists describing precisely how such a system would be built, nor is there scientific consensus on whether or when it might become achievable.

The reason Superintelligence is nonetheless included in serious discussions of AI, despite its speculative nature, is that it anchors an important ongoing conversation within the field about AI safety and alignment, meaning the study of how to ensure that any sufficiently powerful AI system, should one eventually be built, would reliably act in accordance with human values and intentions rather than pursuing goals that diverge from them in harmful ways. For your purposes as a working engineer today, the practical takeaway is simply this: Superintelligence belongs to the far horizon of theoretical discussion, and nothing in your day-to-day engineering work, which will overwhelmingly involve building, deploying, and maintaining Narrow AI systems, requires you to account for it directly, though it is useful to recognize the term correctly when you encounter it in industry discourse rather than mistaking it for marketing language describing a merely impressive current-generation product.

```
   CAPABILITY SPECTRUM (by scope of intelligence)

   Narrow AI (ANI)  ----------->  General AI (AGI)  ----------->  Superintelligence (ASI)
   [ EXISTS TODAY ]                [ DOES NOT YET EXIST,           [ PURELY THEORETICAL,
   one task or a                   no confirmed technical          no known technical
   narrow cluster of                pathway, actively debated       pathway at all       ]
   tasks, no transfer               in research           ]
   of competence
```

## Reactive Machines

Reactive Machines represent the most basic and most historically foundational category along the second classification axis, the one organized around memory and context rather than raw capability, and they are defined by a single stark property: a Reactive Machine responds only to the current input directly in front of it, using no stored memory of any past interaction whatsoever, and it builds no internal model of the world beyond the immediate situation it is reacting to in this exact instant. The classic, frequently cited historical example is Deep Blue, the chess-playing system built by IBM that defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997; Deep Blue evaluated the current state of the chessboard with extraordinary depth and speed, searching through enormous numbers of possible future move sequences, but it retained absolutely no memory of previous games it had played, and it could not learn or adapt its behavior based on past matches, because each new position was evaluated entirely fresh, as though no history existed before it.

In practical, modern engineering terms, you can recognize a Reactive Machine by a very simple test: if you feed it the exact same input twice, completely independently, with no conversation or session connecting the two calls, it produces the exact same output both times, because it has no internal state carried between calls at all; this makes a Reactive Machine architecturally identical, from your backend perspective, to a pure, stateless function, which is precisely why these systems are often the simplest to deploy, scale horizontally behind a load balancer, and reason about, since there is no session state to manage, replicate, or worry about losing.

```
   Call 1: Input "X"  --->  [ Reactive Machine ]  --->  Output "Y"
   Call 2: Input "X"  --->  [ Reactive Machine ]  --->  Output "Y"   (identical, no memory of Call 1)

   No state is carried between calls — a pure, stateless function.
```

## Limited Memory AI

Limited Memory AI describes the vast majority of practical, modern AI systems in production today, and it sits directly between the memory-less Reactive Machines just described and a fully self-aware, continuously learning system that does not exist in practice. A system in this category does retain and make use of some window of recent, relevant information, whether that information is the last several messages of an ongoing conversation, the recent driving history and sensor readings a self-driving car uses to predict the trajectory of a nearby vehicle, or the recent browsing behavior a recommendation engine factors into its next suggestion, but critically, this memory is temporary and bounded rather than being a permanent, ever-growing record that the system carries forward indefinitely and learns from on its own, the way accumulated human experience genuinely reshapes human behavior over a lifetime.

A large language model engaged in a conversation is the example you, as a backend engineer, will encounter constantly, and it is worth understanding the mechanism precisely: the model does not possess some internal, persistent memory store that silently remembers you from one conversation to the next; rather, the entire visible conversation history up to a certain length, called the context window, is explicitly re-submitted alongside every single new request, and the model's apparent memory of earlier messages in that same conversation is entirely a product of that history being fed back into it as input each time, not of any internal state the model itself retained between calls. The moment that conversation ends and a new one begins, or the moment the conversation exceeds the model's fixed context window size, that earlier information is genuinely and permanently gone from the model's perspective unless some external system, very often a database or vector store that you yourself would be responsible for building, deliberately persists and reintroduces it.

```
   Turn 1:  [ System Prompt + Message 1 ]                    --->  [ Model ]  --->  Response 1

   Turn 2:  [ System Prompt + Message 1 + Response 1         --->  [ Model ]  --->  Response 2
              + Message 2 ]           ^
                                       |
                            the entire history is resent
                            each time — this is the "limited
                            memory," bounded by the context window
```

## Generative AI Systems

Generative AI Systems form the final category on the functionality axis, and as you already learned in considerable depth in the very first guide in this series, they are distinguished by their ability to produce new, original content, whether text, images, audio, or code, rather than merely analyzing existing input in order to classify it or extract a decision from it. In the specific context of this classification scheme, organized around how a system relates to memory and the world, it is worth noting explicitly that Generative AI Systems are not a separate tier sitting above or below Reactive Machines and Limited Memory AI in any strict hierarchy; rather, nearly every Generative AI System you will encounter and build with in practice, including the large language models and image generation systems referenced earlier, is architecturally a Limited Memory system, because it relies on the context window mechanism just described to maintain the appearance of an ongoing, coherent interaction across multiple turns.

What makes this category worth calling out as its own distinct functional type, separate from the general concept of Limited Memory AI, is the nature of its output rather than its relationship to memory: a fraud detection model and a chatbot might both technically rely on a similarly limited, bounded context, yet the fraud detector produces a narrow classification decision, while the chatbot produces open-ended, newly generated content that did not exist anywhere in that exact form before the moment of generation. As an engineer building production systems today, you will find that the overwhelming majority of new AI features your organization asks you to build, from drafting customer support responses to generating code to summarizing documents, fall squarely into this Generative AI Systems category, layered on top of the Limited Memory mechanism, which is precisely why understanding context windows, conversation state, and how to responsibly persist and retrieve memory beyond that window has quickly become a core, practical backend engineering skill rather than a purely academic concern.

```
                     FUNCTIONALITY AXIS, SHOWN AS A SPECTRUM

   Reactive Machines  --->  Limited Memory AI  --->  Generative AI Systems
   (no memory at all,       (uses a bounded,          (a Limited Memory system
    e.g. Deep Blue)          recent context window,    whose output is newly
                              e.g. most production      generated content,
                              ML and LLM systems)       e.g. a chatbot or
                                                          code assistant)
```

## Closing Note

Carry forward the two-axis structure from this guide as a permanent mental filing cabinet: whenever someone describes a new AI system to you, first ask where it sits on the capability axis, and the honest answer today, for every system you will actually build, will almost always be Narrow AI, with AGI and Superintelligence remaining theoretical reference points rather than engineering realities; then separately ask where it sits on the functionality axis, which tells you something far more immediately actionable, namely whether you are dealing with a simple, stateless Reactive Machine you can scale like any other pure function, or a Limited Memory system whose context window and conversation state you will need to design, persist, and manage carefully, with Generative AI Systems representing the specific, increasingly common case of a Limited Memory system whose job is to produce brand-new content rather than a narrow decision. Keeping these two questions separate, and asking both of them deliberately every time, is what will let you evaluate any new AI system or vendor claim you encounter with genuine engineering clarity rather than being swept along by loosely used buzzwords.
